MPSim · Material Property Simulation

MPSim computes the effective properties of semiconductor materials from their microstructure. The microstructure is generated on a voxel RVE, the governing equations are solved on it, and the local fields of heat, current, stress and flow are visualised.

How it works

MPSim carries out the workflow of a digital-material study in one browser workspace. It runs on a single PC or on a server accessed from the network; data do not leave the machine.

1

Build the microstructure

Fillers of any shape, size distribution (normal, log-normal or a measured particle-size file) and orientation, pores, coatings, hollow particles, multi-filler blends and thin films, dispersed evenly or unevenly (a region, a gradient, clusters), sheared or compressed while the resin flows, on a voxel representative volume element (RVE) whose size is checked against measured criteria. Every dimension of a particle can have a distribution of its own, and the sizes the structure holds are set against the input in every dimension; the particles are written as files that redraw the structure voxel for voxel. A structure can also be read from a TIFF image (CT, FIB-SEM, segmented labels); its touching particles are separated, and its viscosity is computed from them. A particle list (particles.csv of an earlier run, or a table of spheres) draws a structure again without generating it.

2

Solve the physics

Validated open-source solvers (periodic finite volumes, FFT-accelerated voxel finite elements, NASA PuMA, PoreSpy and openEMS) compute the effective tensors, which are compared with rigorous bounds.

3

Evaluate the fields

Interactive 3D and 2D views of every field, animated field lines, publication figures and a single-file HTML report; every number states the method that produced it.

Analyses

MPSim targets the materials of semiconductor packaging, devices and process equipment: moulding compounds, underfills, thermal interface materials, conductive adhesives, dielectrics, EMI absorbers, porous ceramics and filters.

Conduction

Thermal and electrical

  • Conductivity tensors, interfacial and contact resistance, electrical contact resistance of pastes
  • Finite volume on voxels, or finite elements on a tetrahedral mesh fitted to the particles (the same values as FEniCSx on the same mesh)
  • Percolation of conductive fillers
Electromagnetics

Dielectric, magnetic, EMI

  • Dk, Df, field enhancement, permeability
  • Shielding effectiveness with a full-wave check
Mechanics

Elasticity, CTE and testing

  • Stiffness, engineering constants, CTE tensor
  • Thermal stress; α1 / α2 above Tg
  • Tension or compression test by displacement, force or pressure: force-displacement line, stress fields, load at the matrix strength
Rheology

Viscosity and flowability

  • Viscosity of uncured compounds, flow curves
  • Particle dynamics of the fillers: particle-size effect and shear thinning, on CPU or GPU
  • Maximum packing, underfill filling time, settling
  • Compression of the RVE in 3D while its resins flow: the strain and von Mises stress of each region, pores that close
Multiphysics

Coupled analyses

  • Thermal stress and warpage of a film from the heat flowing through it: temperature field, per-voxel thermal strain by Tg, laminate release free, on a substrate or held flat
  • Joule heating with a temperature-dependent resistance, up to thermal runaway
  • Cure of the resins while the RVE is pressed: Kamal–Sourour kinetics, Castro–Macosko viscosity, gel stop
Reliability

Moisture

  • Moisture diffusivity and saturated uptake
  • Uptake curve of a part, hygroscopic swelling (CME)
Porous media

Flow, filtration, acoustics

  • Permeability, filtration efficiency, tortuosity
  • Acoustic absorption, radiative extinction
Structure

Morphology

  • Porosity, size distributions, porosimetry
  • Pore networks, grain statistics, percolation paths
  • Grain analysis: volume-equivalent and circumscribed diameters by number and by volume, every dimension against the input
Inverse problems

Calibration

  • Unknown material values from measured properties, including viscosity
  • Credible intervals, identifiability, next measurement
  • Every parameter set solved, and the reason when no value reaches a measurement
Studies

DOE and optimisation

  • Factorial and Latin-hypercube studies, filler shares; every case on the Domain as set, one fixed RVE edge or one per case
  • Linked inputs: one factor sets several entries (x, 0.8·x, sqrt(x1·x2))
  • Realised filler content and shares reported for every case
  • Gaussian-process surrogates, NSGA-II
Studies

Sensitivity analysis

  • 25 measures on a study: correlation, regression and ANOVA, dependence, machine learning with SHAP, Sobol, PCE, eFAST, Morris, DGSM, PAWN
  • Five interaction measures, partial dependence and ALE
  • A consensus weighted by each model's cross-validated Q²; every response at once

Application example: a mould underfill from flow to cure

One compound is followed through the analyses relevant for a moulded underfill (MUF): epoxy filled with fused silica of 15 µm and 3 µm at 55 vol%. The viscosity of the uncured compound is computed by particle dynamics. The same particle dynamics shears a random packing in the resin, and the resulting arrangement is taken as the cured microstructure, on which heat conduction, thermal expansion, stiffness, permittivity and moisture uptake are solved (60 µm RVE, 240³ voxels). The presets MUF · epoxy + silica 15 + 3 µm 55 vol% and MUF · from flow to the cured part reproduce all results.

Viscosity of the uncured compound

The particle dynamics resolves lubrication between neighbouring particles, frictional contacts and van der Waals attraction; every particle translates and rotates in a periodic box sheared by Lees–Edwards boundaries, and the relative viscosity follows from the stress carried by the particles. The viscosity overshoots while the shear microstructure forms and reaches a steady value from a strain of about 6; it is averaged over the second half of the run: μr = 24 ± 3, the uncertainty being the expected scatter between independent packings. With the shear-thinning resin the compound reaches 33 Pa·s at 10 s⁻¹.

Microstructure after shear flow

The random packing is sheared with the flow along x and the velocity gradient along z to a whole-number strain (10), at which the sheared periodic images coincide with the ordinary ones. The number of close pairs (surfaces within 5 % of the sum of the radii) increases from 1.7 to 3.8 per sphere, and the pairs orient along the compression axis of the shear (135° from the flow direction) while the extension axis (45°) is depleted, as known for sheared non-Brownian suspensions (Morris, Rheol. Acta 48, 909 (2009)). The contents of the drawn structure equal the input (38.50 and 16.50 vol%).

Thermal conductivity

Steady heat conduction is solved with periodic finite volumes along x, y and z (as computed with 5.10.2; since 5.12.0 the thermal conductivity is solved on the tetrahedral FEM by default). The conductivity through the gap, 0.596 W/m·K, is 3.0 times that of the resin, 9.5 % above the Maxwell–Garnett estimate (the lower Hashin–Shtrikman bound, 0.544) and well below the Bruggeman estimate (0.685): at a conductivity ratio of 7 the points where particles touch carry little of the heat. The shear brings particles into contact and raises the conductivity by 3.5 % through the gap and 4.0 % along y (0.7 % along the flow), of the order of the scatter of one realisation. Refining the grid from 240³ to 360³ changes k_z by −0.04 %.

Thermal expansion and stiffness

FANS solves six unit strains and a uniform temperature change. The volumetric mean CTE, 16.5 ppm/K, lies below the Kerner estimate for separated spheres (22.6 ppm/K), since touching particles restrain the resin; the Hill modulus, 15.0 GPa (ν 0.25), lies within the Hashin–Shtrikman bounds (9.4–30.2 GPa). Fewer close pairs lie along the flow direction x, where E is lowest and α highest; the difference (up to 11 % in α, 5 % in E) is comparable with the scatter of one realisation. For a temperature rise of 100 K with the RVE held at its size, the von Mises stress concentrates in the fine filler and where particles touch (mean 23 MPa, 99th percentile 85 MPa in the 3 µm silica).

Permittivity and dielectric loss

A quasi-static complex conduction solution gives Dk 3.845 in all three directions. The permittivities of silica (3.8) and epoxy (3.9) differ by 2.6 %, so that the Hashin–Shtrikman bounds coincide and the local field departs from the applied field by at most 2.9 %. The dissipation factor, 0.0068, equals the volume-weighted mean of the phases: fused silica leaves Dk unchanged and lowers Df in proportion to the resin it replaces.

Moisture uptake and swelling

Diffusion is solved on the water activity, which is continuous across the phase boundaries; the silica does not absorb water. The effective diffusivity is 0.64 times that of the resin, 18 % below the Maxwell estimate for impermeable spheres (0.78), because the diffusion paths wind between closely spaced and touching particles. The silica restrains the hygroscopic swelling to 0.20 % at saturation.

Finite elements on a mesh fitted to the particles

A voxel solver sees a particle as the voxels it holds: a staircase surface, and a contact wherever two voxels touch. The conduction-type properties (5.9.0) and the stiffness and thermal expansion (5.10.0) can also be solved by finite elements on a tetrahedral mesh that follows the particles' exact surfaces. The voxel solvers stay the default.

The same compound, on voxels and on the fitted mesh

  • The lattice of the voxel corners is cut into tetrahedra, and every tetrahedron is cut where it crosses a particle's exact surface; a film of resin between two particles thinner than a voxel is carried as an interface element of its width.
  • Alumina 45 vol% with BN platelets 5 vol% in epoxy: the finite-volume conductivity falls from 4.69 to 3.64 W/m·K over a four times finer grid and is still falling; the FEM moves from 1.66 to 1.55, from above as conforming elements do (5.12.0).
  • Since 5.12.0 the thermal conductivity is solved on this mesh by default, at every filler content: two touching particles meet across a resin film, with the interface and contact resistances on their true surfaces.
  • A filler five times finer than another needs no finer grid: with the small spheres 3 voxels across the FEM is within 9 % of its value on the finest grid.
  • The same elements as FEniCS, on Windows: on the mesh MPSim writes, FEniCSx (DOLFINx 0.11) gives the same conductivity to twelve digits (1.16977753126), and one sphere per periodic cell converges with the square of the voxel size to Rayleigh's solution.

Stiffness and thermal expansion on the fitted mesh (5.10.0)

  • The same mesh with displacement elements: the six strains and the thermal load are solved together on all cores; a film of resin between two particles is an interface element with the stiffness of a thin layer held between two stiff faces.
  • Fused silica 60 vol% in epoxy: the voxel elements give 24.8 → 16.2 GPa from 32³ to 192³ and are still falling, the FEM 15.4 → 14.8 GPa from 32³ to 64³; the thermal expansion 10.3 → 15.6 ppm/K against 16.0 → 16.6. Extrapolated to an infinitely fine grid both reach 14.1-14.2 GPa and about 17 ppm/K (5.12.0).
  • On the same mesh FEniCSx gives the same stiffness and thermal stress to twelve digits.
  • Against a measurement: glass spheres in epoxy (J. C. Smith, J. Res. Natl. Bur. Stand. A 80A (1976) 45, doi:10.6028/jres.080A.008) at 23 / 30 / 40 / 50 vol%: measured 5.08 / 6.01 / 7.94 / 12.09 GPa, FEM 5.13 / 6.32 / 8.22 / 11.22, voxel elements of the same grid 5.82 / 7.62 / 10.68 / 16.16.
  • Bonded contacts: particles that were sintered or pressed together are joined by a neck of the radius entered. AlSiC with 63 vol% SiC (measured 8.21 ppm/K): 9.23 without necks, 8.20 with a neck of 0.6 of the particle radius.
  • Every FEM result is drawn in 3D on the mesh itself, the voxel solver's field beside it with the same camera and colour scale.
  • In the 3D view (5.11.0) a run solved on the mesh opens in the FEM mesh style: particle surfaces and RVE faces are the element faces, each in its element's colour, with the element edges on request; every phase has its own opacity, and Solo shows one filler of a blend through the others.

A graded mesh for blends of very different sizes (5.10.0)

  • An octree whose cells are as small as the particle they describe, cut at the exact surfaces; its finest cell may be smaller than the voxel, so thin fibres and fine fillers keep their shape.
  • Grains of 3 µm with 0.6 vol% fibres 20 times thinner: 3.6 times fewer tetrahedra than the lattice mesh of the same finest cell, 14 times fewer one level finer. Dense blends whose fines fill every gap save 15 to 31 %: the count follows the surface area, not the mesher.
  • The multigrid solve runs on all cores: a conduction solve at 64³ in 7 s (15 s in 5.9.0).
  • Compression with contacts and friction: the spheres of a compression are also pressed by particle dynamics with lubrication films of their real width and Coulomb friction - 11.5 / 25 / 35 times the plain resin's stress after 30 % for a friction coefficient of 0 / 0.25 / 0.5 at 50 vol%.

Sensitivity analysis: which inputs move a property

The finished cases of a DOE study are asked by every family of method in common use which inputs move a response - alone, through curvature and together with other inputs - and the answer is read from their agreement (5.11.0).

Four inputs of an alumina-filled epoxy, 24 cases

  • Alumina content 20-50 vol%, particle diameter 6-14 µm, filler conductivity 5-35 W/m·K and matrix conductivity 0.15-0.35 W/m·K varied together over a Latin hypercube; the thermal conductivity of every case.
  • The content leads in 24 of 25 measures and takes part in 75 % of the variance (Sobol S_T); the matrix conductivity follows (consensus 0.44), then the filler's (0.25) and the diameter (0.17). The 25 rankings agree with Kendall's W = 0.80.
  • The filler's conductivity is invisible to a plain correlation (r = 0.13, p = 0.54) because the content moves k far more; held fixed, the others no longer hide it (partial rank correlation 0.79, p = 2×10⁻⁵).
  • Content × filler conductivity is the interaction three of five measures find (ANOVA p = 0.005): the filler's conductivity matters more the more filler there is. Six models are scored by cross-validation and the consensus weighs each by its Q² (quadratic surface 0.94, Gaussian process 0.91, random forest 0.72).

Coupled analyses

Since version 5.6.0 MPSim couples heat to stress, current to temperature and cure to flow. Each example below is a preset, run as loaded on an 18-thread laptop.

Thermal stress and warpage of a moulded underfill on a die

A 300 µm moulded underfill (8 µm silica, 50 vol%) on a 300 µm Si die, stress-free at the cure temperature of 175 °C, with the die side at 125 °C and the top at 95 °C. The heat flowing through the film sets the temperature of every voxel, every voxel expands by its own strain, and laminate theory releases the film bonded to the die (70 × 70 × 375 voxels, 168 s). The film warps by −12.89 µm over 10 mm with the edges up; at its uniform mean temperature it would warp by −11.97 µm, so the gradient adds −0.92 µm.

Joule heating of an Ag-filled adhesive joint

A 25 µm joint of 5 µm silver spheres (45 vol%) touching both electrodes carries a current density stepped up to 3·10⁸ A/m²; the silver's resistance rises with temperature (TCR 0.0038 1/K, from R. A. Matula, J. Phys. Chem. Ref. Data 8, 1147 (1979), doi:10.1063/1.555614). At 2.5·10⁸ A/m² the joint settles at 426.5 °C, only 1.7 K above its faces: the current crowds through the contacts, but the path to ambient sets the temperature. At 3·10⁸ A/m² the steady state lies above 1000 °C; as one resistor the joint runs away above 3.2·10⁸ A/m² (70 × 70 × 50 voxels, 81 s).

A moulded underfill that gels before the mould closes

Silica 5 µm (40 vol%) in epoxy pressed at 1 %/s towards 55 % in a mould at 175 °C while the resin cures (Kamal–Sourour kinetics of a moulding compound from E. Franieck et al., Polymers 13, 1734 (2021), doi:10.3390/polym13111734; the Castro–Macosko viscosity rise of a neat epoxy from S. K. Romberg et al., ACS Appl. Polym. Mater. 6, 4874 (2024), doi:10.1021/acsapm.4c00607). The axial stress rises from 1 Pa to 2.8 kPa as the conversion approaches the gel point; the compound gels at 69 s with the mould closed by 49.9 % (70³ voxels, 230 s).

Verification

The engine is verified against problems with known solutions. Every run also reports its own checks (RVE size, resolution, particle count, convergence and rigorous bounds) as passed, warning or failed, together with the reason; a structure that misses its loading is not solved. The particle generator is audited at 40-70 vol% for every shape and size distributions of CV 0.2-0.5 on 256³-400³ grids: every case reaches its loading within 2 %, its mean sizes within 2 % of the input (3 % with only 800 particles in the box).

Download and install

PC without internet

Download MPSim-…-offline-win64.zip from the latest release. It contains the official Python runtime, every wheel including NASA PuMA, and openEMS. Unzip to a short path such as C:\MPSim, run 1_INSTALL_OFFLINE.bat, then 2_RUN_LOCAL.bat.

PC with internet and conda

git clone https://github.com/HyoseokByun-opt/MPSim.git C:\MPSim
cd C:\MPSim
1_INSTALL.bat
2_RUN_LOCAL.bat

User guides, 333 pages in English and 316 in Korean: English (PDF) · Korean (PDF). Each simulation chapter describes how the property is measured, how MPSim computes it and how the results are read.
