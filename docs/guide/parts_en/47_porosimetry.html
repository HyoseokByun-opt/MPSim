22. Pore-size distribution (porosimetry)

Open-source code: PoreSpy morphological drainage

How large the pores are, and how many of each size. It imitates in the computer the standard experiment, mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP), so the result comes out in the same form as a measurement.

22.1 How it is measured — mercury intrusion

Mercury wets almost no material, so it does not enter unless pushed. The method uses this.

  1. The specimen is immersed in mercury. At low pressure the mercury does not enter.
  2. Raising the pressure, mercury enters the largest pores first.
  3. Higher pressure pushes it into smaller pores.
  4. The volume of mercury intruded is recorded at each pressure.
d = −4 γ cos θ / p　(Washburn equation)

At pressure p the pores of diameter d are entered, so the pressure–volume curve is the pore-size distribution. γ is the surface tension of mercury, θ the contact angle (usually 130–140°).

Mercury intrusion has a well-known limitation — the ink-bottle effect. When a wide chamber is reached only through a narrow throat, mercury fills the chamber at the pressure that opens the throat, and the chamber's volume is recorded at the throat's size. Small pores seem more numerous than they are, and this calculation has the same property — it imitates the same physics.

22.2 How the simulator calculates it

It does not compute pressures but does the geometrically equivalent thing.

  1. Take a ball of radius r.
  2. Find every place in the pores where it fits (distance transform ≥ r).
  3. Fill only those reachable from outside through connected space — the part that reproduces the ink-bottle effect.
  4. Reducing r step by step accumulates the volume filled at each size.

Because it asks "can it get in from outside", it gives the same result as the intrusion process of the experiment.

22.3 Reading the numbers

ItemMeaning and use
d10 / d50 / d90 The 10 %, 50 % and 90 % points of the cumulative distribution. d50, the median pore diameter, is the most quoted; 2.46 µm in example B.
Mean pore diameter Volume-weighted mean diameter.
Critical / threshold diameter The diameter at which mercury begins to cross the specimen — the value best correlated with the permeability, close in meaning to the bubble point (chapter 21).
Distribution width Width of the distribution, e.g. d90/d10. Narrow means uniform pores; wide means very mixed sizes.

Read it as one reads the measured curve.

  • A steep step means many pores of that size.
  • Two steps mean two families of pores of different size (a bimodal pore structure).
  • A curve that does not reach 1 means trapped pores unreachable from outside.

22.5 Assumptions behind the calculation

AssumptionWhere reality may differ
Cylindrical pores The Washburn equation assumes cylinders. The experiment makes the same assumption, so comparison is fair, but both differ from the real shape.
Ink-bottle effect kept Not a limitation but a choice to match the experiment. For true chamber sizes, see the pore network.
The structure does not change Real mercury intrusion reaches high pressures (up to 400 MPa) and crushes weak specimens. The calculation has no such effect and may even be more accurate for soft materials.
Voxel resolution Pores smaller than two or three voxels are not caught. Always suspect the small end of the distribution.
No contact angle or surface tension A pure geometric calculation; no mercury properties enter. When comparing with measurements, remember that their diameters depend on the contact angle assumed.

22.6 Reading the rendering — the size at which mercury enters

Compare it with the local thickness (chapter 20). Even a wide chamber behind a narrow throat fills only at the throat's diameter, so the values of this map are smaller than or equal to the local thickness. Chambers where the two maps differ greatly are ink-bottle pores — the part read as smaller pore sizes than real in a measured intrusion curve.
