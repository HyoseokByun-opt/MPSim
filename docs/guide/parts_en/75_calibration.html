30. Calibration — finding unknown properties from measurements

Own implementation: acoustic and diffuse mismatch theory (AMM/DMM) · effective-medium pre-check (Every / Hasselman–Johnson) · RVE surrogate (Gaussian process) · Bayesian posterior · identifiability analysis (Brun et al. 2001) · next-measurement suggestion (D-optimal)

A specimen of epoxy with 50 vol% alumina measures 1.4 W/m·K. What is the interface thermal resistance between filler and resin? This feature works back from measured effective properties to unknown material values automatically. What theory can settle is left to theory, only the rest is fitted to the measurements — and it tells whether the answer is unique.

30.1 Why it is hard — the answer may not be unique

Finding one interface resistance Rint from one conductivity measurement is not hard. The trouble starts with two or more unknowns. The conductivity kp of a powdered alumina, for example, may be well below the single-crystal value (about 30 W/m·K) and is then unknown too. In the dilute limit, however, the conductivity of the compound does not see these two values separately but only in the combination (Hasselman–Johnson 1987)

kp* = kp / (1 + 2 Rint kp / d)

A large kp with a large Rint and a small pair of both can give the same kp*. With one measurement and two unknowns, the answer is not a point but a curve (a valley). Adding measurements at other contents only measures the same kp* again and does not narrow it. A measurement at another particle size d, by contrast, changes only the influence of Rint and separates the two (the method of Every et al. 1992). This feature judges such situations automatically.

The most important principle is therefore to reduce the unknowns. The resin conductivity is measured on the resin alone; values settled by data sheets, literature, theory or MD/DFT calculations are fixed in the Case. Leave only the one or two values truly unknown, and the measurements can determine them.

30.2 What theory can settle first

Interface thermal resistance has theoretical reference values: the acoustic mismatch model (AMM) and the diffuse mismatch model (DMM), which give the fraction of phonons crossing a perfect interface from the sound velocities (from E, ν, ρ) and heat capacities of the two materials (Swartz, Pohl 1989). The Calibration tab computes them automatically from the materials of the Case.

Example: epoxy / Al₂O₃ValueMeaning
DMM9.6×10⁻⁸ m²K/WResistance when phonons scatter randomly at the interface
AMM2.5×10⁻⁷ m²K/WResistance when the interface is smooth and reflects and transmits like a mirror
Kapitza radius0.019 µmRint × kresin. When this length approaches the particle radius, the interface dominates conduction.
Critical d0.038 µmParticles smaller than this conduct heat worse than the resin itself (Hasselman–Johnson).

Real interfaces, with poor adhesion, silane layers and micro-voids, have resistances larger than or equal to these. They therefore serve as a lower limit or the centre of a prior (use as prior: centred on the DMM value, one decade wide). An interface resistance computed by MD or taken from the literature goes in as a centre value the same way. This program does not run MD/DFT itself — it provides the place to accept their results as priors.

30.3 How to use it

  1. Build the Case as the reference. Set the matrix, filler, structure and RVE size as usual; this is the basis of every measured sample.
  2. Enter the measurements (Add measurement): the measured property (thermal or electrical conductivity, permittivity, modulus, CTE), the direction (mean or x/y/z), the value and its uncertainty (±%), and, with Condition, whatever differs from the Case (content, particle size, RVE size …). When the particle size differs a lot, change the RVE and voxel size too, to keep the voxels per particle similar.
  3. Choose the unknowns (Add unknown, at most 3): range and scale (log/linear), prior (uniform in the range, or centre ± width).
  4. Check identifiability: the effective-medium model tells within a second whether "these measurements can determine these unknowns". Check the problem set-up before solving any RVE.
  5. Calibrate on the RVE: solves the real RVE repeatedly to find the answer. Progress appears in the console and the Solver convergence tab.
  6. Read the result (30.5) and put only the determined values into the Case with Apply to the case. Values with only an upper or lower bound, or not determined, stay out.

30.4 How the simulator calculates it

  1. Each sample structure is generated once. The unknowns change only material values, so the structure stays. Structures identical to a previous run in the same work folder are reused.
  2. Design runs: every sample is solved on the RVE at 13 combinations within the ranges of the unknowns (for two unknowns). They are independent and run on several cores at once.
  3. Surrogate: for each sample, a Gaussian process fits the log of the result as a function of the unknowns (log scale) (Kennedy, O'Hagan 2001).
  4. Posterior: prior × likelihood on a fine grid (161×161 for two unknowns). The likelihood includes the measurement uncertainty, the error of the RVE itself (2 % by default) and the surrogate error.
  5. Refinement: more RVE solves where the posterior concentrates and the surrogate is uncertain, until the surrogate error at the estimate is below 0.2 times the measurement uncertainty.
  6. Verification solves: every sample is solved again directly on the RVE with the values found — the "RVE at the estimate" of the result table.
  7. Identifiability and next measurement: the sensitivity matrix of the measurements to the unknowns at the estimate shows which combinations are determined; among candidate measurements with other sizes and contents, the one adding the most information is suggested.

30.5 Reading the results

Example: epoxy with 30 vol% of an unknown filler (true values kp = 2.0 W/m·K, Rint = 2×10⁻⁷ m²K/W), two samples with particle sizes 0.5 µm and 4 µm. The "measurements" are virtual (±3 %), made by solving the RVE with the true values — a problem whose answer is known.

LabelMeaning
DeterminedThe measurements fixed this value. The large number is the maximum a-posteriori estimate (MAP), beside it the 95 % interval and how many times narrower it is than the prior.
Upper bound / Lower boundThe posterior does not fall to 0 at one end of the range — flat to the end, or a peak with a low tail running to the end (2.5 % or more of the probability in the last 10 % of the range). Example: Rint ≤ 1.8×10⁻⁷ — below it the interface barely affects the result and cannot be told from "no resistance". The most probable value is shown too, but only a limit can be stated (the four-specimen result of 32.2).
Not determinedThe measurements do not respond to this unknown, or it trades off with another. The number shown has no meaning.

A sample deviating by more than 2σ means no value within the ranges explains that measurement. Widen the ranges, or suspect causes missing from the model — filler agglomeration, voids, interphases, a size distribution different from the real one. A value forced to fit in such a case must not be used. The "Effective medium" column puts the same values into the effective-medium model; its difference from the RVE (4–8 % here) is the effect of the particle arrangement.

The joint posterior tells the most in this feature. Gathered like a point, both are determined; a long valley means the two trade off along it. The example shows, besides the dark point (the true values), a faint branch with a very small Rint and kp ≈ 1.2: the interpretation "almost no interface resistance and a filler of lower conductivity" is not entirely excluded within ±3 % measurements, which is why the 95 % interval of Rint reaches down to 3.7×10⁻⁹.

ItemMeaning and use
Collinearity index γ How similarly the unknowns act on the measurements (Brun, Reichert, Künsch 2001). Close to 1 they act differently and can be determined separately; above 10–15 they are not identifiable — the effect of changing one is cancelled by another.
Combination of the unknowns The combinations of unknowns (linear combinations of their logs) actually determined by the measurements, with their precision. The example's "0.831·ln Rint − ln kp: ×/÷ 1.15" means this combination is fixed within ±15 %. Combinations marked not determined are set by the prior.

For each candidate measurement it shows the predicted value, the information added (by determinant) and by how much the uncertainty of each unknown shrinks — so the most useful condition can be chosen before making new specimens. To separate the interface resistance, as here, a sample with smaller particles is most effective, because the interface weighs more in small particles. The suggestions are computed with the effective-medium model, for thermal conductivity and Rint·k unknowns.

30.7 The same material measured at several contents

The most common experimental data are several specimens of the same filler and resin at different contents (e.g. 20, 35, 50 vol%). Enter them like this:

  1. Build the Case for one of them (e.g. 35 vol%).
  2. Add each measurement as a row with Add measurement; for the other contents change only …: content under Condition. Use the scatter of repeated measurements as the uncertainty.

The calculation then proceeds as follows:

  • An RVE structure is generated for each content (once each, then reused). A different content changes the distances and contacts between particles, so the same structure cannot serve.
  • At each combination of unknowns all specimens are solved together, and a surrogate is built per specimen.
  • The likelihood is the product over all measurements: the answer sought explains every content with one set of values. Specimens with smaller measurement uncertainty weigh more.
  • Each specimen is solved again with the values found and its deviation shown. If one content alone deviates strongly, a phenomenon acting only there — particle contact at high content (Rc), agglomeration, voids, percolation — is missing from the model, and the result warns about it.

A complete example finding Rint and kp from three specimens (20, 35, 50 vol%) and one of another particle size — specimen structures, what becomes determined as measurements are added, the result screens and what the interface resistance does to the heat flow in the structure — is in section 32.2.

In summary:

  • With one unknown, measuring several contents is the best approach. The interval narrows, and whether one value fits every content is itself a check of the model.
  • With two unknowns (Rint and kp), contents alone are not enough: in the dilute limit the conductivity sees only the combination kp/(1 + 2Rintkp/d) (30.1). Particle interactions at high content give slightly different information, so kp is partly determined, but Rint only gets an upper bound.
  • One more specimen of another particle size separates them. What to measure next in the result puts the same conclusion (a smaller size, 0.15 µm) at the top.
  • When high-content specimens with touching particles are included, the contact resistance Rc acts too. Then make Rint and Rc both unknowns, and let low-content specimens with hardly any contact determine Rint and high-content ones Rc.

30.8 Points of caution

The values found reproduce the measurements "within this RVE model". If the real specimen has agglomeration, voids, a different size distribution or a filler surface layer that the model lacks, the difference is absorbed into the unknowns. For a specimen whose conductivity is high because of agglomeration, for example, the interface resistance found comes out smaller than the real one. Make the structure (size distribution, content, porosity) as close to the measured one as possible.
  • Enter honest uncertainties. ±0.1 % for a value with ±2 % repeatability makes the intervals unrealistically narrow and ignores the model error. Adjust the RVE model uncertainty (2 % by default) to the RVE size and the number of realisations (chapters 6 and 31).
  • Results stuck at a range limit must be checked again with a wider range. There is no need to widen into physically meaningless ranges (negative resistance, k above the single crystal).
  • At most 3 unknowns. Determining more from a few effective-property measurements is nearly impossible; design more independent measurements (other sizes, other properties) first.
  • In highly filled specimens with touching particles, Rint and the contact resistance Rc act together. To find both, measure low-filled samples with hardly any contact (Rint only) together with highly filled ones.
