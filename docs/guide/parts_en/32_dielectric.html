9. Permittivity and loss

Default: periodic finite volume (FV) · optional: NASA PuMA periodic finite elements — the equation of heat conduction, with the permittivity as the property

The values that decide how much a signal slows down and how much of it is lost when this material is an insulating layer — basic properties of package substrates, antenna substrates and capacitor design.

9.1 Why it matters

What Dk doesWhat Df does
Sets the propagation speed of a signal. The delay grows with √Dk, so a high Dk slows signals — why high-speed substrates look for a low Dk. The fraction of the signal lost as heat. The insertion loss grows roughly with Df·√Dk·f, so at high frequencies Df decides.
In capacitors it is the other way round: higher is better, more capacitance per area. Must be low in capacitors too: it means heating and lost efficiency.

9.2 How it is measured

MethodDescription
Parallel-plate capacitor
(ASTM D150)		 The basic method. The specimen between two electrodes forms a capacitor whose capacitance C and loss tanδ are measured with an LCR meter or impedance analyser; the ratio to the capacitance in vacuum is Dk. This is the experiment the calculation imitates.
Dielectric spectroscopy
(BDS)		 Sweeps a wide frequency range to follow Dk and Df; used to study relaxation.
Resonator methods
(SPDR, cavity)		 Used in the GHz range; obtained from the shift of the resonant frequency and Q. The most accurate at high frequency.
Measured Dk and Df depend on the frequency, which is why datasheets always say "at 1 MHz" or "at 10 GHz". This calculation does not distinguish frequencies — it treats the entered phase properties as the values at some frequency and calculates once. Enter the phase properties at the target frequency to obtain the result at that frequency.

9.3 How the simulator calculates it

Exactly like the heat conduction of chapter 7, with the potential in place of the temperature and the permittivity in place of the conductivity.

  1. A potential difference is applied across the RVE; the other faces are periodic.
  2. The potential and the electric displacement inside are solved.
  3. The mean displacement gives the effective permittivity.
  4. Three directions give the tensor.

How the loss (Df) comes out — not a simple average

The loss of a composite is not the volume average of the tanδ of its phases. It is decided by where the energy of the electric field actually resides. Less field enters a phase of high permittivity, so its loss counts for less than its volume fraction.

The panel therefore says Dissipation factor Df (energy-weighted) — the loss weighted by the field energy. This is possible because the solved field gives the field energy in each phase exactly.

9.5 Bounds and models

Locating example A's 5.426 in the chart:

  • Wiener bounds 5.016 – 5.857 — absolute limits set by the volume fractions alone. Inside.
  • Hashin–Shtrikman bounds 5.464 – 5.622 — narrower limits for isotropic composites. The value is 0.7 % below the lower bound.
  • Maxwell-Garnett / Mori–Tanaka 5.464 — a model of non-interacting particles (equal to the HS lower bound for spherical fillers).
  • Bruggeman (EMA) 5.536 — a model weighting the interaction between particles heavily.

The value lies slightly below the HS lower bound because this example uses a coarse grid — 3 µm particles with 0.4 µm voxels (7.5 voxels per diameter, Fast quality). FV tends to come out slightly low on coarse grids (table in 7.2), and the extrapolated value of the resolution check, 5.481, falls inside the bounds. The program records a value outside the Wiener bounds as failed and more than 1 % outside the HS bounds as a warning; smaller departures are differences a finite voxel RVE can produce and pass. For numbers to report, solve again at Standard quality or above.

9.6 Assumptions

AssumptionWhere reality may differ
Quasi-static The field is computed like an electrostatic field, not a propagating wave — valid only when the wavelength is much longer than the RVE. Not at tens of GHz and above.
Properties independent of frequency No relaxation (Debye) behaviour; one calculation at the entered values.
No interfacial polarisation (Maxwell–Wagner) Charge accumulating at the boundaries between phases, which raises Dk strongly at low frequencies, is not modelled — a common reason for a measured low-frequency Dk above the calculation.
No conduction loss Df contains the dielectric loss of the phases only; losses by leakage current are separate.
Perfect interfaces No voids or interphase between filler and resin. In reality such a layer changes both Dk and Df.
