15. Permeability

Open-source solver: NASA PuMA Stokes-flow finite elements — on the connected pores only

How easily a fluid passes through a porous material. The basic property of filters, membranes, sintered metals and porous ceramics.

15.1 How it is measured

A pressure difference Δp is applied across the specimen and the flow rate Q coming out is measured. Darcy's law gives the permeability.

Q / A = (K / μ) · (Δp / L)

A is the cross-section, L the thickness and μ the viscosity of the fluid. The permeability K has the unit of an area (m²) and is a property of the structure alone, independent of the fluid. The darcy, from petroleum engineering, is also common: 1 darcy ≈ 9.87×10⁻¹³ m².

What matters in the experiment matters in the calculation too — at too high a flow rate Darcy's law breaks down. Inertia appears, the pressure loss is no longer proportional to the velocity, and the measured value is not the true permeability. The Forchheimer coefficient below tells where that limit lies.

15.2 How the simulator calculates it

  1. Only the connected pores are kept. Isolated pores cannot be entered by the fluid and are excluded. Because this distinction decides the result, the open and closed porosities are shown together.
  2. The Stokes equations are solved in the pores — slow flow dominated by viscosity, the same condition under which Darcy's law holds in the experiment.
  3. On the walls the no-slip condition: the fluid velocity is 0.
  4. The mean of the velocity field and the applied pressure gradient give K. Three directions give the tensor.

15.3 Reading the numbers

ItemMeaning and use
K tensor (m², darcy) Permeability per direction. Example B gives about 3.7×10⁻¹⁴ m² (0.038 darcy).
Connected (x / y / z) Whether the pores cross the RVE in that direction. With ○, K = 0: a high porosity lets nothing through if the pores are not connected.
ItemMeaning and use
Static flow resistivity σ = μ / K. The key input of sound-absorber design (chapter 18), in Pa·s/m².
Kozeny–Carman constant φ³/(K·Sv²) — the constant of the classical formula that explains permeability by porosity and specific surface; 4–5 for simple structures. Example B gives 8.03: the pores are more winding, with narrower throats, than a bundle of tubes.
Hydraulic tortuosity ⟨|u|⟩/⟨u∥⟩ — the ratio of the distance the fluid actually travels to the straight distance. Larger means more detour.
Open-pore specific surface Surface area of the open pores per unit volume, tied directly to the pressure loss and the adsorption performance.
Forchheimer coefficient β Tells where Darcy's law breaks down at higher velocities. Below the "velocity at which inertia contributes 10 %" shown beside it, the flow is in the Darcy regime. A correlation, not a separate solve.
Klinkenberg slip factor b Gases slip at the pore walls and pass more easily than liquids — why a gas-measured permeability is higher than a liquid-measured one; this is the correction.
Field-line tortuosity Tortuosity from the mean length of streamlines actually drawn through the RVE.

15.4 Seeing it with streamlines

The Streamlines button shows in 3D the paths the fluid actually takes. What is seen here says more than the numbers.

  • If the streamlines crowd into a few wide channels, only part of a high porosity actually carries the flow.
  • Large stagnant zones (where no streamline passes) lower the efficiency of filtration or reaction.

15.5 Assumptions behind the calculation

AssumptionWhere reality may differ
Slow laminar flow (Stokes) Inertia is neglected. At high velocities the real pressure loss is larger; check the limit with the Forchheimer coefficient.
Newtonian, incompressible A fluid of constant viscosity; not valid for non-Newtonian fluids such as polymer solutions.
No slip at the walls Rarefied gases (very small pores or low pressure) slip; the Klinkenberg correction gives the size of the effect.
The structure does not change No pores collapsing under pressure, no clogging by deposited particles.
Single-phase flow Two-phase flow (water and air together) is not solved.
Permeability is proportional to the square of the pore size, so representing the pores accurately matters above all. When the narrowest throats are only a few voxels wide, the permeability goes far off. If the Verification shows a resolution warning, suspect this value first.

15.6 Reading the renderings — the paths the flow takes

The permeability analysis keeps the velocity field u in the pores. It is solved for unit conditions (pressure gradient 1 Pa/m, viscosity 1 Pa·s), so the mean velocity over the whole volume is the same number as the permeability K (m²). Below is example B (porous Al₂O₃, 45 % pores, Kzz ≈ 3.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ m²).

  • Red channels — most of the flow passes through a few wide channels. The permeability is set not by the porosity but by the widest connected channel (the velocity scales with the square of the channel width).
  • Blue pores — connected, but off the flow direction or dead ends. They count in the porosity but carry almost no flow.
  • Streamlines — the paths the fluid actually takes from the top face to the bottom. Where they gather is the main channel; their mean length ÷ the thickness is the hydraulic tortuosity.
