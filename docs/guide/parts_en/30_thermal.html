7. Thermal conductivity

Default: periodic finite volume (FV), solved once · optional: NASA PuMA periodic finite elements (FE), comparison of the two

The quantity most looked at for heat-spreading materials. This chapter explains not just k but every number that appears in this section of the Result Viewer.

7.1 First, how thermal conductivity is measured

A simulated value is easiest to understand once it is clear what the experiment measures. The laboratory obtains the thermal conductivity in two ways.

(1) Measured directly — steady-state methods

A temperature difference across the specimen drives a constant flow of heat, which is measured, and Fourier's law

q = −k · (ΔT / L)   →   k = q · L / ΔT

gives k. The guarded hot plate (ASTM C177) and ASTM D5470, common for TIMs, belong here. The difficulty is the contact resistance: the fine gaps between instrument and specimen add their resistance to the specimen's own, so the specimen is measured at several thicknesses and its resistance is taken from the slope.

(2) Obtained indirectly — transient methods (laser flash)

The more common route in practice. The front of the specimen is heated by a short pulse and the rate at which the back warms up gives the thermal diffusivity α (ASTM E1461). The conductivity is not measured but calculated as

k = α · ρ · cp

so obtaining k by laser flash requires the density ρ and the specific heat cp separately. The Thermal diffusivity and Volumetric heat capacity described below correspond to this relation.

In short — steady-state methods measure k directly, transient methods measure α and calculate k. The simulator solves a steady-state problem for k directly and shows α calculated back from k. When comparing with laser flash, compare α with α: fewer assumptions are mixed in.

7.2 How the simulator calculates it

It repeats in the calculation what the experiment does.

  1. Every voxel gets the conductivity of its region: filler voxels the k of the filler, matrix voxels that of the matrix.
  2. A unit temperature gradient is applied in one direction (for example ΔT = 1 K along x), and the steady heat equation ∇·(k∇T) = 0 is solved, one voxel per computational cell.
  3. The solution gives the heat flux q in every voxel. The ratio of the volume average of q to the applied mean gradient is the effective conductivity in that direction.
  4. Repeating for x, y and z fills the 3×3 tensor.

Boundary conditions: periodic

An experiment cuts a specimen and holds its two faces; the calculation joins the opposite faces of the RVE — it looks at one piece of an endlessly repeated material. This removes the artificial effect of cut faces, and even a small RVE converges quickly to the value inside the material. PuMA's own finite-volume calculation fixes the temperatures of two faces and is therefore not the default (it remains a Solver option).

Two discretisations — FV (default) and FE

The same voxel grid can be solved two ways. The periodic finite volume (FV) method keeps one temperature per voxel, and heat passes only where a voxel shares a face with its neighbour (harmonic mean of the two conductivities). NASA PuMA's periodic finite elements (FE) keep a temperature at every voxel corner; a corner on a boundary belongs to filler and matrix alike, so heat also crosses between voxels that touch only at an edge or a corner. FE therefore crosses narrow gaps and contacts easily, as if the filler were half a voxel larger, and comes out higher the coarser the grid.

Which is right was checked on problems with known answers. A simple cubic array (one sphere per periodic cell) has a small cell and can be solved with up to 256 voxels per diameter; at that resolution FE and FV extrapolate to the same value within 0.1 %. With that as the reference, the errors at ordinary resolutions were measured.

Comparing the two methods (optional)

Also solve with the other method and compare solves the same problem once more with the other method and shows both. As in the table above, the true value often lies between them, so their difference is the size of the discretisation error. It roughly doubles the conduction time and is off by default. When the two differ by more than 5 % the check list warns; usually the gaps or contacts between particles are drawn with only one or two voxels (5.5).

Resolution check (automatic)

When gaps between particles are not fully drawn and particles touch on the grid, the same particles are drawn again on a grid 1.5× finer after the analysis and the first direction is solved once more (positions, sizes and orientations are stored as continuous coordinates, so the structure is unchanged). The result shows both values, the change, and a value extrapolated assuming an error proportional to the voxel size; a change above 3 % raises a warning. FV is fast, so this usually costs a few seconds.

The convergence test of the iteration

The original stopping test of the MINRES that PuMA uses (‖r‖ / (‖A‖·‖x‖)) loosens as the grid grows. On a 50 vol% structure with the default tolerance it stopped at values +0.4 % high on 64³, +7.5 % on 128³ and +32 % on 192³. Since v4 the test is the relative residual ‖r‖ / ‖b‖, independent of grid and units, and a diagonal (Jacobi) preconditioner reduces the iterations (192³: 1418 → 468, same value). The self-test checks this on two grids (chapter 31).

7.3 Reading the numbers

k tensor (W/m·K)

xx, yy and zz are the effective conductivities for heat flowing along x, y and z. Similar values mean isotropy; one larger value means the filler is aligned, or a connected path has formed, in that direction. The Isotropic mean below is the mean of the three, to compare with a measurement that does not distinguish directions.

Thermal resistivity (m·K/W)

The reciprocal of the conductivity, for when it is easier to think in resistances (series stacks, for example).

Area-specific thermal resistance (mm²·K/W)

The value TIM specifications actually use — the thermal resistance per area of a layer of thickness L,

R″ = L / k

It takes the thickness entered in Layer thickness, so it changes with that thickness. This is what ASTM D5470 yields — except that the measured value includes contact resistance, while this value is the resistance of the material alone. That is the most common reason why the calculation comes out lower than the measurement.

Thermal diffusivity (mm²/s)

It says how fast heat spreads. By definition

α = k / (ρ · cp)

calculated from the k just solved and ρ·cp as the volume-weighted mean of the densities and specific heats of the phases. Where the conductivity says "how well heat passes in the steady state", the diffusivity says "how fast a change of temperature travels". A material can pass heat well (large k) and still respond slowly, if it stores much heat (large ρcp). It is directly comparable with α measured by laser flash.

Volumetric heat capacity (MJ/m³·K)

ρ·cp — the denominator of the relation above: the heat needed to warm a unit volume by 1 K.

Anisotropy (xx+yy)/2 : zz

The ratio of the in-plane mean to the through-thickness value. Above 1, heat flows better in-plane. Platelet fillers (h-BN, graphite) raise it strongly — a drawback when heat must cross the thickness.

Field-line tortuosity / Field lines that cross the sample

The result of actually tracing the field lines of the heat flux. The mean length of the lines that cross the specimen over the straight distance is the tortuosity; close to 1, heat flows straight. Few crossing lines mean the heat is channelled through a few paths.

7.4 Checking that the value makes sense

Chapter 25 explains this chart in detail. In brief — Wiener and Hashin–Shtrikman are bounds no physical material can cross, so a calculated value outside them is an error. A difference from approximate models such as Maxwell-Garnett or Bruggeman is not an error but the effect of the microstructure: the models know nothing of orientation or contacts.

7.6 Interfacial thermal resistance (Rint, Rc)

Between filler and matrix, and between two touching fillers, phonons crossing the interface meet a resistance (Kapitza resistance, m²K/W). It is entered in the phase editor of the Structure tab (section 5.7).

InputMeaningTypical values
Rint filler–matrixThe whole surface of the filler (or its shell)10⁻⁹–10⁻⁷ m²K/W (boundary conductance 10⁹–10⁷ W/m²K)
Rc filler–fillerFaces where two particles touch — between different fillers per pair (5.7)Often taken larger than the interface (point contacts, surface oxides)

When the values are unknown, the Calibration tab (chapter 30) shows the Rint of a perfect interface by the acoustic and diffuse mismatch theories (a lower bound) and finds Rint from measured conductivities.

How it is calculated

The resistance sits directly on the voxel faces. A face between different materials a and b has the conductance 1/kface = 1/(2ka) + 1/(2kb) + R/h (h the voxel size), and Rc/h is added on faces where particles touch. It needs faces, so with any resistance present the conduction is solved by the built-in periodic finite-volume solver (shown as fv-interface in the Solver record of the Result Viewer). The staircase surface of the grid is larger than the real surface (about 1.5× for a sphere), so R is multiplied by the ratio of real to grid area, keeping the total interface conductance Atrue/R. The share of the thermal energy dissipated at the interfaces appears separately under energy share in the Result Viewer.

Verification

A dilute dispersion of spheres (diameter 20 voxels, 3.8 vol%, kp/km = 20) was compared with the analytical solution of Hasselman–Johnson. For Biot numbers α = Rintkm/a = 0, 0.5 and 2 they agree within 0.26 % (area correction 1.51 — the theoretical value for a sphere is 1.5). Rc on touching faces equals the series law to numerical accuracy. Both checks are in the self-test (chapter 31).

Hasselman–Johnson:   k/km = [kp(1+2α) + 2km + 2φ(kp(1−α) − km)] / [kp(1+2α) + 2km − φ(kp(1−α) − km)],   α = Rintkm/a
The critical radius ac = Rint km. At α = 1 (with kp ≫ km) the filler looks as if it had the conductivity of the matrix; below this size, adding filler lowers the conductivity. The analysis handles particle sizes directly, so the effect can be seen by varying the diameter at constant content.

ArrowsIn-plane direction of the heat flow (hot top → cold bottom). Arrows bending into the particles show the particles "pulling in" the heat. Their length follows the square root of the magnitude (26.2).
  • Temperature section — inside a particle the colour hardly changes (isothermal), and the temperature changes in the matrix between particles. How much the colour bands bend near the particles is the effect of the filler; straight horizontal bands mean the filler contributes nothing to the conduction.
  • Field lines — the paths the heat actually takes from the top face to the bottom face. How far they swerve to find particles is the Field-line tortuosity of the results (mean path length ÷ thickness). Lines crowding along certain paths mean those paths (particle chains) carry most of the heat; straight, even lines mean the matrix carries it.
What to look at first — ① where and how many red spots (bottlenecks) there are, ② where in the matrix the temperature drops, ③ whether the field lines crowd along particle chains. The three together answer "why this k". When the directions differ, choose the field of that direction in Displayed quantity and compare.
