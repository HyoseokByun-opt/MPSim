11. EMI shielding

Open source used: scikit-rf transmission-line theory — combined with σ, εr and μr of the composite

How many dB a plate of this material blocks. One point to note — this is not a separate electromagnetic analysis: it converts the three properties of chapters 8–10 into the shielding of one plate.

11.1 How it is measured

MethodDescription
Coaxial transmission line
(ASTM D4935)		 The standard method. A disc specimen in a coaxial fixture; a network analyser measures the transmitted signal (S21). SE = −20·log₁₀|S21| (dB). Widely used because the specimen can be small.
Shielded room The material on a real enclosure; field strengths inside and outside are compared. Close to real use, but needs large specimens.
Free space The specimen between horn antennas; suited to high frequencies (GHz).

11.2 How shielding happens — three mechanisms

SEtotal = R + A + M　(dB)
MechanismPrincipleGrows with
Reflection (R) When the impedances of material and air differ, the wave bounces off the surface and does not even enter. Higher conductivity. Dominant at low frequencies.
Absorption (A) The wave that enters decays as it turns into heat. Greater thickness and larger σ·μ. Dominant at high frequencies.
Multiple reflections (M) Bouncing back and forth inside, part of the wave comes back out. It can be negative (it reduces the shielding). Matters only in thin plates; vanishes in thick ones.
The difference between reflection and absorption matters in practice. A wave stopped by reflection goes elsewhere and causes problems there. Materials that stop waves by absorption are therefore preferred, and that needs magnetism (μr), not just conductivity — the reason for magnetic fillers such as carbonyl iron.

11.3 How the simulator calculates it

In two steps. First the microstructure (RVE) is solved for the effective properties of the composite; then a plane wave meeting a plate of thickness t with those properties at normal incidence is transmitted and reflected according to the exact formulae (transmission line, Schelkunoff). Since v4 the first step is done at every frequency separately.

Complex homogenisation per frequency (default)

  1. Every material gets its complex admittivity at the frequency f: κ = σ + jωε₀εr(1 − j tanδ) — conduction and displacement current together.
  2. The RVE is solved directly with these values (the finite-volume method of the conduction analysis in complex numbers, in the two in-plane directions x and y). The real part of the result κeff(f) is the effective conductivity σ′(f), the imaginary part the effective permittivity ε′(f).
  3. This is repeated at 10 frequencies between fmin and fmax (Solved frequencies) and interpolated logarithmically in between. The permeability μeff is the static value of the magnetic analysis.
  4. σ′(f), ε′(f) and μeff give the reflection, absorption and multiple reflections of the plate.

The static method (up to v3, selectable under Method) solved the DC σ and the static ε separately and combined them, missing the effects where the two currents mix.

  • Metal particles kept apart — at radio frequencies metal particles behave as perfect conductors and raise the permittivity of the composite. A static permittivity analysis uses the metal's εr (usually 1) as it is and comes out lower instead.
  • Weakly conductive fillers (carbon, semiconducting fillers) — charge piling up at the interfaces and relaxing, the interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner) polarisation, makes ε′ and σ′ change with frequency. The static method has no such change.
  • A connected conductive network — the conduction current dominates and both methods give the same value.
Every frequency solves two in-plane directions in complex numbers, about 20 conduction solves more in all. With a connected network, ε′ is below a millionth of σ′ and is not resolved ("—" in the table); it does not affect the shielding.
The shielding is right only if the results of chapters 8–10 are right. The electrical conductivity in particular is decided by percolation (chapter 8): if the filler is not connected, the shielding comes out close to zero as well.

11.4 Reading the numbers

ItemMeaning and use
SE total (dB) Total shielding. 20 dB = 99 % blocked, 30 dB = 99.9 %, 40 dB = 99.99 %. Practical targets of commercial shielding materials are usually 30–60 dB.
SE reflection (R)The part blocked by reflection.
SE absorption (A)The part blocked by absorption; it grows in proportion to the thickness.
Skin depth δ The depth at which the wave falls to 1/e. A plate thicker than 3δ blocks enough by absorption. δ shrinks rapidly as the frequency rises.
Impedance ratio Impedance of the material over that of air. The larger the difference, the more reflection.

Reading the example

Example C2 gives 99 dB at 1 MHz. Example C, with the same filler at only 30 vol% and the particles kept apart, gave 0 dB. Five vol% apart, and the results split completely for one reason — in C2 the filler is connected, in C it is broken. It shows that in shielding materials connectivity matters more than content. The σ of C2, however, is sensitive to how the particle contacts are drawn on the grid (16 % change in the resolution check, 46 % spread between directions), and the dB values waver accordingly.

Do not take numbers above 100 dB at face value. Example C2 shows more than 2000 dB at high frequency (10 GHz); the absorption term grows exponentially with thickness over skin depth, and the value comes out of the formula. In reality
  • the dynamic range of measuring equipment usually ends at 100–120 dB, and
  • real enclosures leak far more through seams and apertures than through the material.
Read above 80 dB as "sufficient as a material" and use the digits beyond only for comparison. The real performance is decided by design and assembly.

11.5 Assumptions and limits

AssumptionWhere reality may differ
The properties of each material do not depend on frequency The frequency dependence the composite has because of its structure (interfacial polarisation, conductor-like metal particles) is in the frequency-resolved analysis of 11.3. The dispersion of each material itself — the permeability of magnetic fillers dropping sharply above a few hundred MHz (Snoek limit), εr(f) of dielectrics — is not, because the library holds one value. GHz values of magnetic fillers are overestimated.
Quasi-static approximation The RVE is much smaller than the wavelength, and conductive particles are thinner than their skin depth; eddy currents inside particles are not computed. The Validity of the results gives the frequency where this breaks down — for the carbonyl iron of example C2 (σ 107 S/m, μr 50) the skin depth falls below the particle size from a few hundred MHz on.
Infinite flat plate No edges, curvature, apertures or seams — where most real shielding failures happen.
Plane wave, normal incidence A plane wave from afar is assumed. The near field (right beside an antenna, say) behaves differently.

In short, the result is for comparing candidate materials and judging their level. It cannot replace certified values; the final judgement belongs to an ASTM D4935 measurement.

11.6 Checking with a full-wave simulation (openEMS) — experimental

Full-wave check with openEMS places the RVE itself as a plate and solves Maxwell's equations in the time domain, voxel by voxel (openEMS, FDTD). This result, which involves no homogenisation at all, is compared at 10–100 GHz with the shielding of the same plate obtained by homogenisation. openEMS comes with the offline installation (tools\openEMS); a check takes several minutes and is off by default.

SettingDescription
DomainA unit cell of the lateral size of the RVE. Perfect electric conductor (PEC) walls at the x faces, perfect magnetic conductor (PMC) walls at the y faces — the symmetry planes of a normally incident plane wave (E along x). The homogenised value for comparison is solved with the same walls (it may differ slightly from periodic conditions).
GridInside the plate one voxel = one FDTD cell. Outside, cells growing by 1.3×; Mur absorbing boundaries at both ends.
ExtractionVoltage probes before and behind the plate; S21 (shielding) and S11 (reflection) from the ratio to a reference run without the plate.

Verification so far

Structure (plate 20 µm thick)Largest difference (10–100 GHz)Interpretation
Homogeneous plate (εr 4, σ 2000 S/m)0.001 dB, |S11| 1e-4The openEMS set-up and extraction are exact (a self-test check).
Weakly conductive filler 20 vol%, particles apart (σ 1 S/m)0.003 dB, |S11| 2 %The frequency-resolved homogenisation, interfacial polarisation included, agrees with the full-wave result.
Conductive filler 35 vol%, particles apart (σ 104 S/m)0.3 dB, |S11| 0.32 vs 0.21Shielding agrees; FDTD reflects a little more — the materials of boundary voxels are averaged differently.
Conductive network 35 vol%2.4 dBFDTD conducts slightly better.
Conductive spheres 35 vol%, contacts allowed6.5 dBFDTD treats voxels touching only at an edge as connected (it averages the four cells around), the face-based homogenisation as broken — the same property as the difference between FE and FV (7.2). A large difference means the result is sensitive to how the particle contacts are represented.
Carbonyl iron (σ 107 S/m, μr 50)Not comparableThe skin depth (0.2 µm) is smaller than voxels and particles, outside the validity of both analyses, and FDTD cannot resolve more than 80 dB. The program says so.
In short, with particles kept apart the frequency-resolved homogenisation agrees well with the full-wave analysis. Where particles touch at the voxel scale, the two represent the contacts differently and differ, and that difference is the uncertainty of the result. This is an experimental feature still under review.

11.7 Renderings — EMI has no field of its own

The EMI shielding is calculated for a layer from the effective properties (σ, ε, μ) of the electrical, dielectric and permeability analyses, so EMI itself has no 3D field. Why the effective properties take their values is seen in the figures of the conduction and magnetic analyses.

  • Reflection loss (SER) and absorption loss (SEA) are governed by σ, which the current-density picture of chapter 8 decides — whether a connected conducting path exists (example C has none and SE close to 0; example C2 has one and a high SE).
  • Absorption by magnetic fillers is governed by μ; why μeff comes out far below the filler's own μ is seen in the magnetic-field picture of chapter 10 (H crowding into the gaps).
When explaining EMI results, show the 3D current-density picture (chapter 8) with the spectrum. The connected red path is the direct evidence of "why it shields".
