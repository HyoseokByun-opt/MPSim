12. Elasticity and CTE

Solver: FANS (the periodic Q1 voxel finite elements of NASA PuMA, solved by FFT-preconditioned conjugate gradients — default since v5) · NASA PuMA FE (optional). Six strains + one temperature load = seven load cases

Many reliability problems of semiconductor packages come from joining materials whose thermal expansions differ. This analysis obtains the elastic moduli and the thermal expansion of the composite together, and shows the stresses that arise inside when the temperature changes.

12.1 How they are measured

Coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE)

TMA (thermomechanical analysis) or a dilatometer measures the change in length while the specimen is heated slowly.

α = (1 / L) · (dL / dT)

Usually in ppm/K (= 10⁻⁶/K). The value changes strongly through the glass transition, so the temperature range always comes with it.

Elastic moduli

From the initial slope of the stress–strain curve in tension or bending (universal testing machine), or from the longitudinal and shear wave speeds measured ultrasonically. The ultrasonic method is better for the small-strain moduli.

12.2 How the simulator calculates them

Instead of pulling in one direction at a time as an experiment does, seven solves settle the whole behaviour at once.

  1. Six strains — three normal (x, y, z) and three shear strains, each of unit size, give the stresses in the RVE; each fills one column of the 6×6 stiffness matrix C*.
  2. One temperature load — with the strain held at zero, only the temperature rises by ΔT. The internal stresses from the phases trying to expand differently give the thermal expansion.

The solver of v5: FANS

Up to v4 the elastic analysis solved NASA PuMA's finite elements by MINRES iterations. On larger grids the iteration count grew with the grid, and on a 500³ grid more than 30 hours were not enough to reach a relative residual of 1e-5. v5 keeps the same elements (periodic Q1 voxel finite elements) but solves them by conjugate gradients preconditioned with the exact inverse, computed by FFT, of the stiffness of a homogeneous reference material (FANS, Leuschner and Fritzen 2018). The inverse of the reference is one 3×3 matrix per frequency, almost free, and the iteration count stays at a few tens per load whatever the grid.

Both solvers solve the same discretisation and agree (C11 within 5e-8; 3.5e-9 in the self-test). Only the speed differs, and since PuMA's iterations grow with the grid while FANS's stay constant, the gap widens on larger grids. One iteration costs in proportion to the voxels, so scaling the measurements to seven loads on 500³ gives about 3 hours for FANS and, with eight times the iterations of 64³, about 100 hours for PuMA (a proportional estimate, not a measurement — consistent with PuMA not finishing 500³ in more than 30 hours). Already at 96³ FANS is 11× faster.

The iteration runs to a relative residual of 1e-6 (1e-5 at Fast quality), where C equals the fully converged value within two parts per million. The self-test checks a homogeneous RVE and laminates (exact to 1e-15), the exact relation for two-phase composites (Levin's theorem), the Hashin–Shtrikman bounds for dilute spheres, iteration counts that do not grow with the grid (24³ and 48³), and agreement with PuMA (chapter 31).

The Elasticity and CTE choices also include an FFT (Moulinec–Suquet) solver. It is a different discretisation and gives different values on coarse grids. For epoxy + 29 vol% stiff spheres the Young's modulus was 8.16/7.62/7.16 GPa with FANS (voxel finite elements) on 24³/32³/48³ and 6.88/6.76/6.62 GPa with FFT; refined, both meet at about 6.3 GPa. Both converge from above, but the voxel finite elements come out much stiffer on coarse grids, and FFT is closer to the converged value. The difference between the two methods shows the discretisation error of that grid; values to report should come from a grid fine enough by the resolution check (chapter 6).

A correction to the elasticity and CTE results up to v4. During the development of v5, the isotropic-material function of PuMA 3.2.2 was found to double the shear modulus of every phase (it uses engineering shear strains while placing 2μ on the stiffness diagonal) and to return the shear axes in reversed order. v5 builds the material matrices itself and corrects the axes. In an epoxy + Al₂O₃ 25 vol% example the v4 values were biased by C11 +12 %, bulk modulus +11 % and thermal expansion −8 %. Levin's theorem checks only that C and α agree with each other and could not catch this; v5 checks the G of a homogeneous RVE and the Hashin–Shtrikman shear bounds separately. Recalculate elasticity and CTE values obtained with v4 in v5.
The rubbery properties above Tg (α2, the hot modulus) come with the same analysis when Also above Tg is on (14.5).

12.3 Reading the numbers

α tensor (ppm/K)

Six components (xx, yy, zz, yz, xz, xy). The first three are the linear expansions in each direction, similar when isotropic. The Volumetric mean below is their mean, to compare with a TMA measurement that does not distinguish directions. The last three (yz, xz, xy) are tensor components αij, close to 0 for a random structure. layer_card.json stores them as the Voigt vector that pairs with C*, so there these three entries are 2αij.

Example A gives 21.7 ppm/K (xx 22.0, yy 21.5, zz 21.7). Epoxy alone is usually around 60 ppm/K, so 40 vol% AlN lowers it to a third — reducing the CTE is one of the main reasons for adding filler. The value agrees with the exact two-phase relation (Levin's theorem) within one part per million.

E, G, ν (per direction)

Young's modulus E (stiffness in tension), shear modulus G and Poisson's ratio ν per direction; anisotropic materials differ between directions. νxy and νyx may differ — not an error but a normal property of anisotropic materials.

Voigt / Reuss / Hill

Three ways to summarise an anisotropic result by one isotropic value: Voigt the upper bound, Reuss the lower, Hill their mean. Use Hill for rough comparison with other data or when one number must be reported.

Universal anisotropy index AU

0 is perfect isotropy; larger is more directional. Spherical fillers placed at random should give close to 0; a large value signals a small RVE or biased particles.

Much stiff filler gives AU of 0.1–0.2 even with random spheres. Example A (AlN 40 vol%) gives 0.14: the normal stiffness along the grid axes exceeds that at 45° (Zener ratio 0.71). It appears to come from voxelised particles and thin matrix gaps lying parallel to the grid axes, not from the real material, and shrinks at lower contents or on finer grids. To pass such a material on as one isotropic value, use the Hill mean; do not read the directional differences of E and G as real anisotropy.

Stiffness C* / Compliance S*

The full 6×6 matrices, in a form that can be entered directly as this material's properties in other programs (structural FEA), linking the scales of a multiscale analysis.

12.4 Thermal stress — the most practical result

The stresses that actually arise inside when the temperature changes by ΔT, per phase. ΔT is set on screen, usually the cooling from the process temperature to room temperature.

ItemMeaning
von MisesThe equivalent stress for judging yield and damage; the maximum matters.
HydrostaticThe hydrostatic part: positive is tension, negative compression. Interfaces debond more easily in tension.
99th percentileThe level of the top 1 %, not the single most extreme point — steadier than the maximum for practical judgement.
A low mean with a much larger maximum means the stress concentrates at certain points — usually narrow gaps where particles face each other closely. Painting the von Mises field on the surfaces in the 3D view shows where (chapter 26).

12.5 Comparison with models

Thermal expansion has classical models too. Turner assumes every phase undergoes the same strain and comes out low; Kerner accounts for shear and comes out higher. The Schapery bounds are rigorous upper and lower limits; a value outside them is an error.

12.6 Assumptions

AssumptionWhere reality may differ
Linear elasticity No plasticity, creep or viscoelasticity. Epoxy is strongly viscoelastic near its glass transition; its behaviour there is outside this analysis.
Perfectly bonded interfaces Filler and matrix do not separate. Debonding would change both stiffness and stresses.
Properties independent of temperature E and α are constant over ΔT; not true over wide temperature ranges.
Starting free of residual stress Stresses already present from cure shrinkage are not included.
Stiffness of pores Pores get a very small stiffness rather than exactly zero (for numerical stability); the ratio is shown on screen.
The elastic analysis solves seven loads and needs the most memory and time of all analyses (about 2.5 kB per voxel). When time is short, run it alone at a lower resolution first to see the trend.

12.7 Reading the renderings — where the stress gathers

The thermal-expansion analysis keeps the thermal stress under the temperature load (ΔT) — von Mises stress, hydrostatic stress σm, and the components σzz and τxz. Below is example A (epoxy + AlN 40 vol%, ΔT = 100 K, constrained).

What is seenMeaningInterpretation
Most particle surfaces blue, red spots here and there (up to about 87 MPa) Stress concentrates where particles touch or come very close. Candidate sites for cracks and interface debonding; compare with the strength of the matrix (epoxy). The more contacts (the higher the content), the more such spots.
σm at −60 to −70 MPa almost everywhere The constrained condition keeps the RVE from expanding, so heating compresses everything. This is the stress of "a layer held tightly by its surroundings and heated". To see only the mismatch between particles and matrix, calculate with the free condition — the mean stress becomes 0 and only stresses of opposite sign in particles and matrix remain.
The meaning of the sign — the matrix α (epoxy, about 60 ppm/K) exceeds the filler's (AlN, 4.5 ppm/K), so on heating the matrix pulls on the particles and puts them in tension, and on cooling (from the cure temperature to room temperature, say) the particles are compressed. Changing the sign of ΔT changes the sign of the stresses. In processing the critical step is usually the cooling from cure to room temperature, so try a negative ΔT.
