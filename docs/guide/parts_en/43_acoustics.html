18. Acoustic absorption

Open-source basis: the structure results fed into the Johnson–Champoux–Allard (JCA) equivalent-fluid model

How much sound a porous material absorbs, frequency by frequency. The results of earlier analyses (permeability, tortuosity, pore size) are the inputs — a typical example of several analyses combined.

18.1 How it is measured

MethodDescription
Impedance tube
(ISO 10534-2, ASTM E1050)		 The standard method. The specimen closes the end of a tube; a loudspeaker sends sound and two microphones separate the incident and reflected waves, whose ratio gives the absorption coefficient α. Normal incidence — directly comparable with this calculation.
Reverberation room
(ISO 354)		 The specimen is laid in a large room and α follows from the change of reverberation time. Valid for sound from all directions, so larger than the impedance-tube value; building-material data sheets usually give this.
Airflow resistance
(ISO 9053)		 Very slow air is passed through and the pressure difference measured. The single most important property for absorption; this program computes it from the permeability (chapter 15).

18.2 How absorption happens

Sound entering the pores moves the air back and forth through narrow passages, losing energy by friction with the walls. Compression and expansion also produce heat that escapes into the walls, so energy is lost as heat too. An absorber must therefore have open pores — closed-cell foam (polystyrene) insulates heat but absorbs almost no sound.

The flow resistivity must be moderate. Too low and the sound simply passes; too high and it cannot enter and is reflected. Real absorbers mostly lie in 5×10³ – 5×10⁴ Pa·s/m². A calculated value far outside this range means the structure cannot serve as an absorber.

18.3 How the simulator calculates it

The JCA model treats the porous material as "an equivalent fluid with viscous and thermal losses". That fluid is defined by five values, all from earlier analyses.

JCA inputWhere it comes fromRole
Flow resistivity σPermeability (chapter 15)Size of the viscous loss
Porosity φStructure analysis (chapter 20)Volume the sound can enter
Tortuosity α∞Tortuosity (chapter 17)Degree of detour
Viscous characteristic length ΛPore shapeFriction in narrow throats
Thermal characteristic length Λ′Specific surfaceHeat loss to the walls

These five give the effective density and bulk modulus; the surface impedance of a layer of given thickness in front of a rigid wall then gives the absorption coefficient.

18.4 Reading the numbers

ItemMeaning and use
α (absorption coefficient) 0 reflects everything, 1 absorbs everything; given per frequency. Example D: 0.02 at 125 Hz, 0.82 at 4 kHz.
NRC Mean of the values at 250, 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz, rounded to 0.05 — the single number most used in building-material specifications. Example D: 0.2.
SAA Mean of 12 values from 200 to 2500 Hz, finer than the NRC.
Surface impedance The acoustic impedance seen at the surface. The closer its real part to that of air (415 Pa·s/m), the better the absorption.
Layer thickness The layer thickness used. It decides the result strongly — see below.

Why low frequencies are not absorbed

Example D absorbs 0.82 at 4 kHz but only 0.02 at 125 Hz — why its NRC is as low as 0.2. Not because the material is poor, but because of the thickness.

For good absorption, the point where the air particles move fastest must lie inside the layer. That point is a quarter wavelength from the wall.

FrequencyWavelengthThickness needed (λ/4)

500 Hz69 cmabout 17 cm
2000 Hz17 cmabout 4 cm
4000 Hz8.6 cmabout 2 cm
For low frequencies there is no way but thickness. This is the most fundamental limit of absorber design: no material catches low frequencies in a thin layer. In practice an air gap is placed behind the layer (same effect), or a resonant absorber is added. Changing the layer thickness and watching the curve move gives a feel for it.

18.5 Assumptions behind the calculation

AssumptionWhere reality may differ
Rigid frame The solid skeleton does not vibrate. Soft foams and fibres actually vibrate with the air and absorb differently near that resonance; this needs Biot theory, not covered here.
Normal incidence Corresponds to impedance-tube measurements. Reverberation-room values (ISO 354) come out larger — take care when comparing with specifications.
Rigid backing An air gap or another layer behind changes the result.
No facing on the surface Nonwovens, perforated plates or films often fixed to real absorbers are not included; they improve low and reduce high frequencies.
Linear acoustics Valid at ordinary sound pressure levels.
The JCA model itself An approximate model summarising the structure in five values; accuracy drops for very inhomogeneous structures.

18.6 Reading the rendering — flow in the absorber

The absorption analysis takes the flow resistivity σ, the tortuosity α∞ and the viscous and thermal characteristic lengths Λ, Λ′ from the flow fields of the permeability and diffusion analyses. Straight, even streamlines mean α∞ close to 1; where the flow speeds up through narrow windows sets Λ (the viscous characteristic length). To change the absorption, changing the size of these windows (strut thickness, pore size) is the most direct route.
