25. Reading the Result Viewer

When the analysis is done, every result is arranged on this screen. There seem to be many items, but the structure is always the same. Learn it in this chapter and you can read analyses you have never seen before.

25.1 Layout of the screen

The contents on the left have four groups:

  • Overview — summary of this run
  • Simulation — property results (thermal, electrical, electromagnetic, mechanical, flow)
  • Structure analysis — quantification of the structure itself (pores, particles)
  • Verification — check results and input conditions

Clicking an item jumps to its section. Only the analyses switched on are shown; an item missing from the screen was not switched on.

25.2 Summary — where to look first

The tiles at the top show only the most important values — density, thermal conductivity, permittivity and so on, depending on the analyses switched on. When comparing several conditions, this row is often enough.

Below them the run time, the solvers used and the library versions are recorded — information kept so that, looking at a result later, one knows under which conditions it was calculated.

25.3 Three things common to every property analysis

(1) Tensor table

A property can differ with direction, so it is given as a 3×3 tensor; the screen shows the diagonal components xx, yy, zz.

  • Three similar values → isotropic: the particles lie at random.
  • One direction larger → anisotropic: particle orientation or connection paths.
  • Isotropic mean — the mean of the three, to compare with measurements that do not distinguish direction.
  • With repeated realisations a 95 % CI appears too. A wide interval signals a small RVE.

(2) Derived numbers

Below the tensor come practical values derived from it: thermal resistance and diffusivity for the thermal conductivity, resistivity and sheet resistance for the electrical conductivity, and so on. Values that need a layer thickness use the thickness entered on screen.

(3) Field statistics per region

The mean and maximum of the computed quantity in each phase (matrix, filler) are tabulated. A maximum far above the mean means local concentration — a clue to where breakdown or cracks start.

25.4 Bounds and mean-field models — is the value plausible?

This one plot tells whether the result makes physical sense.

ItemMeaning
Wiener boundsThe loosest upper and lower bounds, set by the volume fractions alone — series and parallel arrangements.
Hashin–Shtrikman boundsThe tightest rigorous bounds for isotropic two-phase composites. Real values usually lie inside them.
RVE xx / yy / zz (periodic FV)The values actually solved, per direction; the method in brackets.
RVE mean (PuMA FE check)A value solved by another method, shown only when the comparison of both methods is switched on in the Simulation tab.
Maxwell-Garnett / Mori–TanakaClassical models that fit well when particles are sparse.
Bruggeman (EMA)A model better suited to high contents where particles begin to touch.
Reading order — ① is the computed value inside the rigorous bounds (bars)? If not, it is an error, recorded as failed in the Verification. ② Is the change in the resolution check small (and, with the comparison switched on, are the two methods close)? A large change means insufficient resolution. ③ The difference from the model values is not an error but the effect of the microstructure — that difference is the very output of this analysis.

25.6 From the results to the 3D view

At the top right of each section are buttons such as Show field, Streamlines and Show the particle and its path. They jump straight to the 3D view of that result — the fastest way to see what happened inside the structure when the numbers alone do not explain it (chapter 26).
