13. Viscosity and flowability

Solvers: the built-in FANS voxel finite elements (creeping flow, B-bar elements) · Krieger–Dougherty · Farr–Groot maximum packing · Chateau–Ovarlez–Trung non-Newtonian homogenisation

Before cure, underfills (MUF), moulding compounds (EMC), adhesives and pastes are liquids. This analysis finds how much the filler raises the viscosity, how fast the compound fills a narrow gap, and whether the filler settles. The more filler is added to improve the thermal and mechanical properties, the worse the flow — the point of this analysis is to see both on the same structure.

13.1 What it calculates

The viscosity of the filled liquid divided by that of the resin (matrix) alone, the relative viscosity μr.

μr = μcompound / μresin

μr is set by the amount, shape, size distribution and arrangement of the filler alone, independent of the resin's viscosity. Once known it can be multiplied in when the temperature changes the resin viscosity. With little filler it rises slowly, as Einstein's 1 + 2.5φ; close to the maximum packing fraction φm, where the particles can no longer move, it diverges. Choosing a filler content in practice is mostly a question of how far from this divergence one stays.

13.2 How it is measured

MethodDescription
Rotational rheometer
(parallel plate, cone and plate)		 The sample between two plates is sheared and the torque measured; varying the shear rate gives the flow curve (viscosity versus shear rate). Divided by the resin alone it gives μr — the measurement most directly comparable with this analysis.
Capillary viscometer Pushes the sample through a thin tube and measures the pressure drop; suited to high shear rates (moulding, dispensing).
Spiral flow
(EMC)		 The compound is pushed into a spiral mould and the length it flows before curing is measured — a practical index combining viscosity and cure rate.
Underfill flow test The time an underfill needs to fill a given distance in a constant gap between two glass plates; compared with the Washburn time of 13.4.

13.3 How the simulator calculates it

The flow solved on the RVE

Slow viscous flow (creeping or Stokes flow) obeys the equations of an incompressible elastic solid, with velocity for displacement, strain rate for strain and viscosity for shear modulus. It is therefore solved with the FANS voxel finite elements of the elastic analysis. B-bar elements prevent the locking of incompressibility, and the filler is a fluid 1000× more viscous than the resin, practically rigid (the intrinsic viscosity differs by 0.4 % between 1000× and 10000× — a self-test check). A unit shear rate is applied to the whole RVE in the yz, xz and xy planes and the viscosity is obtained from the mean stress; uniaxial extension is solved once more.

Maximum packing fraction φm

  • Spherical fillers (size distributions and blends of fillers included): the Farr–Groot algorithm (J. Chem. Phys. 131, 244104, 2009) gives the random close packing directly from the particle-size distribution, by turning the packing of 3D spheres into the filling of a 1D line with rods. It reproduces the 27 3D simulations of the paper (binary size ratios 1:2 to 1:10) within 0.011 and gives 0.6435 for equal spheres.
  • Non-spherical fillers (platelets, fibres, polyhedra and others): the structure generator packs the same particles until they stop growing. Rotating particles converge very slowly, so the number of steps is limited and the value is shown as a lower estimate.
  • Friction: rough fillers, or fillers without dispersant, jam under shear before random close packing (0.585 against 0.64 for spheres, Boyer et al. 2011). Particle friction = Frictional multiplies φm by 0.585/0.64; the results show both cases.

Intrinsic viscosity [η]

2.5 for spheres (Einstein); Simha's limits for slender fibres and thin platelets. For other shapes an RVE with the same particles diluted to 4 vol% is solved separately and [η] = (μr − 1)/φ.

Which value becomes the result

When the resin films between particles are thinner than one or two voxels, the grid cannot resolve the lubrication and the RVE value comes out too low. Up to 35 vol% filler with particles not touching, the RVE flow solve is the best estimate; above, Krieger–Dougherty with φm.

μr = (1 − φ/φm)−[η] φm    (Krieger–Dougherty 1959)

Non-Newtonian resins, underfill, settling

  • For a shear-thinning resin (power law, Carreau, Cross), the flow curve of the compound follows from the homogenisation of Chateau, Ovarlez and Trung (2008): the resin between the particles is sheared A = √(μr/(1−φ)) times faster than the mean.
  • Underfill filling time: capillary flow in a parallel-plate gap (Washburn), t = 3μL² / (hγ cos θ).
  • Settling: the Stokes velocity times the hindrance of the other particles, (1 − φ)4.65 (Richardson–Zaki).

13.5 Checking the flowability of a MUF

A MUF (moulded underfill) moulds over the chip and fills between the bumps in one step, so the demand for much filler to lower the thermal expansion collides head-on with the demand to flow between narrow bumps. Check in this order:

  1. Material: the MUF resin as the matrix, spherical fused silica as the filler.
  2. Structure: the filler-size distribution (single size, log-normal, or several fillers of different sizes) and the content.
  3. Switch on Simulation → Viscosity and flowability and enter the viscosity model of the resin at the moulding temperature (Newtonian, Carreau, Cross or power law), the reference shear rate (a representative value during moulding, e.g. 10 1/s), the underfill gap (bump height) and the flow length (die size).
  4. After the run, look at the four items below in the Result Viewer.

13.6 Points to watch

Bubbles (air) are calculated without surface tension. The flow solve has no surface tension, so bubbles deform freely and lower the viscosity, to 1 − (5/3)φ in the dilute limit. Small bubbles held round by surface tension (low capillary number) raise it instead, to 1 + φ (Taylor). With bubbles present the checks show this warning.
At high contents (50 vol% and above) φm is almost the whole result. At 60 vol%, changing φm from 0.64 to just 0.70 changes μr from 84 to 30, almost threefold. With one or two measured flow curves, the most reliable use — simpler than Calibration (chapter 30) — is to fit φm to the measurement, as in "φm that puts Krieger–Dougherty through the RVE value", and predict other contents with it.
  • The RVE sees only steady shear viscosity. Thixotropy, time-dependent yield stress and the viscosity rise of curing enter only through the resin model (yield stress, Carreau and so on).
  • Nano-fillers (a few hundred nm and below) raise the viscosity further through Brownian motion and adsorbed surface layers. These effects are not in the calculation, so measurements come out higher at the same content.
  • Touching particles are seen by the RVE as one rigid cluster, and the value becomes too high; Krieger–Dougherty then becomes the best estimate automatically.
