5. Building the structure (Structure)

This tab builds the inside of the material. Choose which fillers, how much of each, their size and shape and how they are placed, and the program generates a three-dimensional structure (RVE) that meets those conditions. v4 added many shapes, and pores and fillers are told apart by their material, not by their shape.

5.1 Adding a phase — shapes

Choose a shape with the Add buttons at the top of the panel or Add a phase in the ribbon. Every shape supports rotation, orientation, size distributions, shells (coating or hollow) and permitted overlap alike.

Choosing the materials of the matrix and the phases

Clicking the matrix row at the top of the list opens the matrix editor (the tab stays). Exactly as for a phase, a library material is chosen in the Material list; with Air as the matrix the phases form a packed bed or a porous solid. The editor's Properties show the material's properties read-only; to change them, Edit this material opens the library in the Material tab (chapter 4). A value changed in the library applies at once to every region that uses the material.

5.2 A pore is a material, not a shape

Up to v3 there were pore-only shapes, "Spherical pores" and "Pore network". Since v4, a phase of any shape whose material is a gas (Air) is a pore. Click Make it pore space (Air) in the editor or choose Air in the material list.

To makeSettings
Isolated spherical pores (closed-cell foam)Sphere + Air
Flat pores, crack-like voidsSpheroid (c/a < 1) or Cuboid + Air
Open connected porosity (filters, membranes, porous ceramics)Network + Air
Skeleton of a sintered metal or ceramic, the solid of a foamNetwork + the solid, matrix Air
Packed bed of particles (powder layer)Matrix Air, particles solid
Because the distinction is made by material, the pore analyses — permeability, filtration, acoustics, porosimetry — work on every gas region (the matrix too, when it is Air). Isolated pores are apart and let no fluid through, so their permeability is 0 — flow needs connected pores (Network + Air, or an Air matrix).

5.3 Content

Either volume fraction vol % or weight fraction wt %. Weight fractions are converted to volume fractions with the densities of the materials, so the densities must be right. The Composition table at the bottom of the panel shows the converted values, the density and the specific heat of the whole.

5.4 Size distribution and orientation

Choosing Log-normal with a coefficient of variation (CV) scales all dimensions of a particle by the same drawn factor. Shapes other than spheres have an orientation.

SettingResult
Random 3DFully random rotation; the result comes out isotropic.
Aligned with x / y / zThe long axis of the particle (its local z axis) is aligned with that axis; the spin about it is random.
Random in the xy planeLying in the plane in random in-plane directions — the most common state in films and sheets.

Orientation spread (°) sets how perfect the alignment is: 0° is perfect, 15–30° is realistic for real processes. Platelets (spheroids with c/a < 1, thin cuboids) aligned with z lie flat in the xy plane.

5.5 Placing the particles — high contents and touching particles

Particles are placed without overlap. The program chooses one of three methods by the conditions.

MethodWhenPrinciple
Growth with overlap relaxation + Monte-CarloAll particles are spheres Small spheres grow while overlaps are pushed apart (the force-biased / collective-rearrangement family). If they stall short of the target, Monte-Carlo compression (Metropolis moves, then inflation to the first contact) takes over. 250 equal spheres: 60 % in 3.5 s, and 0.997 of the target size at random close packing (64 %).
Voxel RSANon-spherical shapes, low to medium contents Particles are inserted one by one at random positions and orientations, and drawn again when they overlap on the actual grid. The overlap test is exact, but no more particles fit beyond about 38 % for spheres and far less for elongated particles (jamming).
Rigid clump relaxationWhen RSA jams before the target Every particle is approximated by a clump of spheres (the multi-sphere method of DEM); the clumps start small and grow while overlaps are pushed apart and the particles move and rotate. Orientation constraints (alignment, in-plane) are kept. At the end the exact shapes are drawn on the grid and the overlaps left at corners and edges the clumps do not cover are removed: every shared voxel pushes the two particles apart along the line between their centres and turns them by the torque it exerts — particles caught at their corners do not come free by pushing, but they do by turning. Whatever is still left is cleared by shrinking the particles involved in steps of 2 %, shape and orientation kept. Particles never overlap and are never trimmed.
How much fits. Random close packing of equal spheres is about 64 %, higher with a size distribution. The random-packing limit of elongated and plate-like particles falls as the aspect ratio grows. When the target exceeds what a shape can reach, the program creates no overlap and reports the size reached (Particle size reached) as a check. Aligned particles pack denser than random ones.
Overlap check of blends. Every generated particle is drawn again on an empty grid, one at a time, and the voxels inside two particles are counted; spheres are also checked by the distance of their centres against the sum of their radii (v5.0.1).

The 40 spheres of a clump cover only 35 % of a platelet and 82 % of a cube, so at high contents the corners of the real shapes dig into each other. Up to v5.0 such a voxel went to the particle placed first, trimming a corner off the other; now the particles are pushed and turned apart.
When the 3D view looks overlapped. A particle crossing an RVE face is drawn cut at the face, as a flat disc. With particles large against the RVE (15 µm particles in a 60 µm RVE, say) there are many such discs, and discs on different faces seen in perspective seem to overlap. A 2D section shows the particles apart (figure below).

Touching particles

Two particles that do not overlap as continuous shapes still become neighbouring voxels on the grid when the gap between their surfaces is narrower than a voxel, and then look as if they touch. This "contact on the grid" can shake a conduction analysis badly: voxel FE (PuMA) conducts heat even between voxels that share only an edge or a corner, face-based FV only through shared faces. Three settings handle this without changing the particle shapes.

SettingBehaviour
Keep apart where the loading allows
(default)		 Particles are moved (never trimmed) so that no voxel touches a voxel of another particle in any of the 26 directions. RSA rejects such positions, and the packings keep a surface gap of 2 voxels (at least √3 voxels rules out corner neighbours). After the clump packing the exact shapes are drawn again and the remaining contact pairs are pushed apart. When the content is too high for the gap, the gap is kept as far as it fits and particles touch only where they must.
Allow contacts Leaves the particles where the packing put them; they may touch. Used when a contact network is intended (with Rc).
Trim contacts to matrix (v3) Turns touching voxels into matrix. The contacts disappear, but the filler shapes are trimmed.

Remaining contacts are reported in the Touching particles check as the number of voxel neighbour pairs (and how many of them share a face); a contact resistance Rc (section 5.7) can act on the faces.

With high-contrast fillers at high contents, the narrow gaps and contacts between particles decide the result. Drawn with only one or two voxels, a gap makes the value depend strongly on the method and the resolution. The default settings (Keep apart + FV) are the combination that minimises this; to check, switch on Also solve with the other method and compare in the Simulation tab to see the difference between the methods, or refine the voxels and see whether the value stays.

A Minimum gap (a multiple of the size) keeps a real gap between particles (particles separated by a dispersant or a coating). Overlap permitted allows overlap — for agglomerates, fibre networks and sintered contacts.

5.6 Coated and hollow particles (Shell)

Coating or hollow shell puts a shell on any shape. The shell is the region between the particle surface and that surface moved outwards along its normal by the thickness (an exact offset for spheres, cylinders, boxes, polyhedra and helices).

  • Surface treatment, coating — a silane layer, a metal plating.
  • Hollow particles — with Air as the phase material and SiO₂ as the shell, the particle is hollow.
The dimensions entered are those of the core inside the shell. The shell is added outside, so the outer diameter is d + 2 × thickness (a polyhedron moves every face out by the thickness). Catalogues usually give the outer diameter of hollow glass spheres: for 20 µm outside with a 1 µm wall, enter d = 18 µm. The content refers to the whole particle, shell included. With a size distribution, Wall scales with particle size keeps wall thickness over radius the same for every particle (hollow spheres of one density grade).

Thin shells demand resolution. The automatic grid sets the voxel size so that the shell thickness holds 2 voxels (conduction analyses), or 4 with elasticity and thermal expansion.

5.7 Interfacial thermal resistance — Rint and Rc

In a real composite there is a resistance to heat crossing between filler and matrix and between two touching fillers (Kapitza resistance, in m²K/W). It is one of the most important corrections of a thermal-conductivity calculation, and it matters more the smaller the filler.

InputWhere it acts
Rint filler–matrixEvery voxel face where the phase (its shell, if it has one) meets the matrix
Rc filler–filler contactVoxel faces where two different particles touch (the faces recorded in section 5.5)
Contact resistance between fillers
(with two or more fillers)		Faces where different fillers touch. It can be set per pair (for example Al₂O₃–AlN); left empty, the mean of the two fillers' Rc is used. Up to v4, Rc acted only between particles of the same filler, and contacts between different fillers were left without resistance.

The thermal-conductivity results list, per filler pair, the Rc applied and the number of voxel faces where that pair touches. In a dense blend of Al₂O₃ and AlN, for instance, contacts between the two fillers can be the most numerous, and then the value of that pair dominates the result. Chapter 30 shows how to find Rint or Rc from measurements.

Both values are used by the thermal-conductivity analysis only, and with any resistance present the conduction is solved by the built-in periodic finite-volume solver. It puts the resistance on every voxel face as 1/kface = 1/(2ka) + 1/(2kb) + R/h — PuMA's finite elements share nodes between neighbouring elements and cannot hold a face resistance. A voxelised surface is a staircase with more area than the real surface (about 1.5× for a sphere), so the program corrects R against the analytical surface area. v3 approximated the resistance by lowering the conductivity of one layer of matrix, which also blocked the heat flowing along that layer; v4 replaced it.

The critical radius. A spherical filler of radius below ac = Rint × kmatrix cannot raise the conductivity of the composite, however conductive it is. For epoxy (0.2 W/m·K) and Rint = 1×10⁻⁷ m²K/W, ac = 0.02 µm; for 1×10⁻⁶, 0.2 µm. The editor shows the critical radius of the current value. When the value is unknown, leave it at 0 (perfect bonding) and read the result as an upper bound. Typical values are 10⁻⁹–10⁻⁷ m²K/W.

5.8 Preview and generation

Generate structure in the ribbon builds only the structure, without solving, and shows it in 3D. Check the content, the particle count, agglomeration, the number of touching faces and the size reached here before moving on to the analyses. The structure is kept in that run, and Run afterwards continues the same run without generating it again (section 2.4) — likewise when analyses are added later.
