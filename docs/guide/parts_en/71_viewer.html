26. Working with the 3D / 2D view

Numbers alone rarely tell "why that value came out". This view shows what actually happened inside the structure.

26.1 What to show — the toolbar

ItemDescription
Displayed quantity (list) The structure (phase colours) and every computed field. Every direction solved is listed separately, so, for example, the heat flux under an X and a Z gradient can be compared in turn.
Colour map (Jet / Turbo / Viridis …) Pick what looks best. For reports, Viridis also survives black-and-white printing.
Colour range (1–99 % / Min–max / Manual) The default 1–99 % keeps extreme values from hogging the colours. When comparing several conditions side by side, set the same range with Manual.
Two exceptions apply automatically. Class numbers (percolation and the like) are not measured values and use the full range — when one class fills most of the volume, a percentile window collapses onto it and the view turns a single colour.
And quantities that change sign (stresses and the like) get colours symmetric about 0, but only when the data actually cross 0. When everything is compressive (negative), as in thermal stress, a symmetric range would waste half the colours, so the data range is used.
Log Switch on when values span tens of times or more (current density and the like) to reveal the structure.
ISO / +X / +Y / +ZSet the view to fixed directions.

26.2 What to draw — the Display panel

SwitchWhen to use it
Particle and pore surfaces Draws the surfaces of particles and pores. On by default — the most natural way to see a structure.
Smooth / Voxels How surfaces are drawn. Smooth gives each particle a smoothed surface; Voxels shows the voxel faces exactly as the solver computed them. See below.
Colour surfaces by the field Paints those surfaces with the computed values — for the temperature or stress distribution on particle surfaces.
Field on the RVE faces Draws the field on the six faces of the cube. Good for the overall distribution, but it hides the interior.
OpacityMakes surfaces translucent to look inside.
Section planes (X/Y/Z)Cut sections to see the interior; the sliders move them.
Vector arrowsArrows show the direction of the flux. Density and length are adjustable (see "Arrows" below).
Field lines / pathsDraws streamlines or particle paths. Streamlines are coloured by the local magnitude (resin velocity, heat flux), with their colour bar at the top right. With Animate on, a dark dot (tracer) moves along each line at the local speed — in resin flow one sees layers sliding and speeding up in the gaps between fillers, in heat flux the channels where heat passes quickly (section 32.1).
Volume renderingShows the whole distribution like a translucent fog.
Pore networkShows the pore network as balls and sticks.
Phase coloursChoose a colour per phase; see below.
Lighting and depthAdjusts the sense of depth; see below.
Particle surfaces are extracted from the solver grid itself. Thinning the grid for display would tear or erase thin shells two or three voxels thick (the glass wall of hollow particles, for instance), so up to a 400³ grid the solver grid is used as it is. Core, shell and matrix all appearing distinct is normal; to look inside, cut with Section planes.

Smooth surfaces and voxels

StyleHow it is madeWhen to use it
Smooth (default) A surface is built for each particle separately (VTK SurfaceNets) and smoothed by at most one voxel from the voxel boundary. Touching particles keep their own surfaces and do not melt into one lump. Most views of structures and field distributions; report figures.
Voxels The voxel faces exactly as the solver computed them, each face coloured with its voxel's value (no interpolation). To check whether particles really touch, how many voxels a gap spans, or whether the staircase of a boundary affects the result.
Early versions built one surface for all particles of a phase. The denser the particles, the more close neighbours fused during smoothing and looked soldered together, and parts with the wrong surface orientation (outside) looked dark like holes. Now surfaces are built per particle number, and the outside of each face is judged from the particle itself. If a section and the 3D view still disagree, switch to Voxels — that is the structure the solver saw. The 3D data of older runs can be rebuilt when the run kept its structure (pipeline.rebuild_view).

Arrows (Vector arrows)

  • Arrows are available only for quantities computed as vectors — heat flux, current density, electric field, magnetic field, velocity. While a scalar such as temperature or potential is shown, the switch is disabled; pick a quantity such as velocity or current in Displayed quantity first.
  • Switching arrows on makes the particles translucent automatically: arrows stand evenly in particles and matrix, and opaque particles would hide most of them.
  • Arrow density is the number of arrows along an edge (about 18 by default); Arrow length is a length factor.
  • By default the length follows the square root of the magnitude. Drawn proportional to the magnitude, the arrows in a poorly conducting matrix (where most of them stand) shrank to a few pixels, and moving the slider seemed to change nothing. Same length for every arrow gives all arrows the same length, showing direction only; read the magnitude from the colour.
  • In 2D sections the in-plane component is drawn, and figures saved with High-resolution figure follow the same rules.

Which side's value is painted on a surface

A surface is the boundary between two phases. Quantities that differ between phases, such as temperature or stress, jump at the boundary, and the picture changes completely depending on which side is read. The program reads by these rules:

QuantitySide readWhy
Most quantities
(temperature, stress, velocity, current density …)		 Just inside the surface — the phase itself A particle surface must carry the particle's value; with matrix values mixed in, one cannot tell which phase it belongs to.
Matrix ligament thickness Just outside the surface — the matrix It is 0 inside particles, so it only makes sense read on the matrix side.
Electric and magnetic field enhancement
(|E|/E₀, |H|/H₀)		 The side with the lower permittivity (permeability) The normal D (B) is continuous at the boundary, so the field always crowds into the lower side. High-permittivity or high-permeability particles lower the field inside themselves and push it into the gaps, so the matrix side is read; where the particle side is lower, as in hollow particles or pores, the inside is read. Reading the other side would erase the very concentration one wants to see.
These rules apply only to 3D surfaces. 2D sections and the RVE faces draw the voxel values as they are, so both sides of a boundary are visible. When unsure which side a value belongs to, check in 2D — hovering shows the phase and the value at that point.

Choosing colours per phase

The colours of matrix and fillers can be chosen directly. The list is built from the phase names of the loaded run, so each case shows its own phases.

A colour is set in one place and applied everywhere — 3D surfaces, 2D section pixels and the legend all change together. Use it to match report figures to a house standard, or to keep phases distinguishable in black-and-white print. Restore the default colours reverts at any time.

Lighting and depth

ItemWhat changes
Ambient Light reaching equally from every direction. Raising it brightens the shadows and flattens the look.
Diffuse Brightness varying with how much a face turns to the light — the main source of depth.
Specular Shiny highlights. A little makes surfaces look solid; a lot makes them look like plastic.
Shade the volume rendering Shading for volume rendering too. Off, it looks like flat fog.
Emphasise edges Draws outlines on the surfaces of each phase — effective for telling touching particles apart, whose boundaries blur with smooth shading alone.
The defaults (ambient 0.18 · diffuse 0.82 · specular 0.28) are a safe choice but can look dull for some structures. When dense particles look like a lump, raise diffuse, lower ambient and switch on Emphasise edges. The sliders act immediately on the current view.
Arrows and paths lie inside the structure. If they are on but invisible, Field on the RVE faces is hiding them. The program switches it off automatically, but check if you switched it on yourself.

26.3 Three view modes

ModeUse
3DThe overall shape and distribution.
2DOne large section — best for reading values precisely.
3D + sectionsBoth at once: the 3D shape and the internal sections together.

What the 2D view can do

  • Hovering shows the coordinates, the phase and the computed value at that point.
  • Clicking moves the other two sections through that point.
  • The wheel zooms, dragging pans, a double click resets.
  • With arrows on, the in-plane flux direction is drawn over the section.

26.4 Saving figures

Two options sit at the right of the toolbar:

  • Save view — saves exactly what is visible as a PNG. Fast.
  • High-resolution figure — re-renders on the server with PyVista, keeping the current view and colour range but much sharper: suited to report and paper figures.

26.5 Good things to check with this view

QuestionHow to look
Is the filler actually connected? Show current density or heat flux in Log and switch on the field lines. If connected, the paths follow the filler.
Where does stress concentrate? Paint the von Mises field on the surfaces; the narrow places between particles turn red.
Which channels carry the fluid? Velocity field + streamlines: stagnant zones and main channels separate.
How large a particle passes? Switch on the percolation path: the particle moves along it at its real size.
Was the structure made as intended? Show the structure (phase colours) only and sweep the sections; clusters and voids become visible.
