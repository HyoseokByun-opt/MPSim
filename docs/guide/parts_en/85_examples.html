32. Worked examples for semiconductor materials

This chapter solves two real material problems from start to finish: what is entered on screen, which figures come out, and how those figures and numbers are read to reach a decision. Every figure and number is an actual result computed with this program.

32.1 Example A — flowability of a MUF (molded underfill)

The problem

A MUF does the moulding over the chip and the underfill between the bumps in one step. Lowering the thermal expansion needs a lot of silica filler; filling between the bumps without voids needs good flow. The goal is to find where these two demands collide.

InputValue
ResinEpoxy, Carreau model at the moulding temperature (μ₀ 2 Pa·s, μ∞ 0.2 Pa·s, λ 0.05 s, n 0.6) — 1.92 Pa·s at 10 1/s
FillerSpherical fused silica: monomodal 5 µm (30 and 55 vol%) / bimodal 15 + 3 µm (70/30, 55 vol%)
AnalysisSimulation → Viscosity and flowability, reference shear rate 10 1/s, bump gap 50 µm, flow length 10 mm
RVEMonomodal 5 µm: 30 µm, voxel 0.375 µm (80³) · bimodal: 60 µm, voxel 0.5 µm (120³)

Result 1 — where and how the resin flows

The viscosity is decided in the end by how hard the resin between the fillers is sheared. The screen shows this in three ways.

In simple shear the upper layers slide towards +x and the lower ones towards −x. The fillers, as rigid bodies, rotate as a whole (circular streamlines inside the fillers), and the resin goes around them and escapes through the gaps. Near the mid-height (the plane of zero velocity) the resin wraps around the fillers in vortices. Where the colour is red — gaps where two fillers face each other closely — the resin is sheared several times harder than average, spends that much more energy, and the compound becomes more viscous.

The dye lines tilt with the shear and bend like steps where they pass a filler. The filler is rigid and does not deform, so to move the same distance the resin between fillers must deform for it too. This shows by eye why fillers raise the viscosity, from Einstein's 2.5φ to the divergence of Krieger–Dougherty.

Animated resin flow in the Result Viewer moves tracers along the streamlines inside the translucent structure in the 3D view. Upper and lower layers slide in opposite directions, tracers at mid-height almost stand still, and tracers passing between fillers speed up and slow down. The lines themselves are coloured by velocity with the colour bar at the top right, so fast and slow layers are distinguishable even in a paused view or a report figure. The "flow" that still pictures convey poorly can be shown in a meeting as it is.

At 55 vol% the picture changes. The resin remains only as a network of thin films between fillers, each strongly sheared (mean amplification 7.0, against 2.0 at 30 vol%). In the same section the dye lines bend almost everywhere. A little more content and the films break, the particles interlock and nothing flows — the maximum packing fraction φm is close. The RVE voxels can no longer resolve the thin films (24,220 particle contacts), so the program uses Krieger–Dougherty as the best value instead of the RVE value (164).

Result 3 — how it looks in the process

Flow front under the die in the Result Viewer moves the flow fronts of resin alone and of the compound side by side in the same gap (Play or the time bar). Capillary flow slows as the wetted length grows (x ∝ √t), so the two look similar at first and then drift apart: when the resin alone has filled at 380 s, the compound is still at 2.6 mm. A real MUF is filled under pressure, but the pressure or time needed is proportional to the viscosity, so this comparison is directly a design comparison.

Conclusion: at 55 vol% the monomodal distribution is on the verge of jamming — high viscosity, very sensitive to lot scatter and surface condition. The same content in a bimodal distribution halves the viscosity and widens the margin. If the content must go higher (e.g. for a CTE target), raising φm with a multimodal distribution comes first, and the largest particles stay within what the bump height allows (the top cut).

32.2 Example B — interface thermal resistance from measurements at several contents

The problem

The thermal conductivity was measured on three specimens of epoxy with 0.5 µm fused silica (20, 35, 50 vol%) and one with 2 µm silica (35 vol%). Unknown are the interface thermal resistance Rint between filler and resin and the conductivity kp of this silica grade (whether the data-sheet 1.38 W/m·K holds for this powder is uncertain). Here the "measurements" were made by solving the RVE with true values Rint = 1.0×10⁻⁷ m²K/W and kp = 1.2 W/m·K plus a scatter within ±1.5 %, so the method can be checked against a known answer. The fifth specimen is the one the program suggested after seeing the first four (step 3).

Step 1 — theory first

The Calibration tab shows the interface resistance of a perfect interface from the sound velocities and heat capacities of the two materials: for epoxy/silica, DMM 3.5×10⁻⁸ and AMM 3.9×10⁻⁸ m²K/W. Real interfaces have larger or equal values, so the range of the unknown is set wide, 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁵, with this value as the reference for the lower limit. The value finally found (about 1×10⁻⁷) is about 3 times the theoretical lower limit — a common size for real interfaces with imperfect adhesion.

Measurements varying only the content cannot fully separate kp and Rint: in the dilute limit the conductivity sees only the combination kp/(1 + 2Rintkp/d). A specimen with larger particles (2 µm), where the interface weighs less, pins kp, but the lower side of Rint stays open: with 0.5 µm particles, Rint = 2×10⁻⁸ gives 2Rintkp/d of only 0.1, indistinguishable from "no interface resistance" within the measurement scatter. One more specimen with small particles (0.15 µm, where the same Rint gives 2Rintkp/d = 1.6), in which the interface weighs heavily, closes the lower side too, and both values are determined in narrow intervals containing the true values.

Step 3 — reading the result screen, and the next measurement

A valley means "these measurements cannot separate this direction". The collinearity index γ (4.0) of the Identifiability box is computed from the slopes near the estimate and does not see a plateau running to the end of the range. The program therefore labels a posterior that does not fall to 0 at an end of the range as Upper bound/Lower bound, stating which end and the most probable value.

Step 4 — one more specimen, as suggested

Entering the measurement of the suggested specimen (50 vol%, d 0.15 µm), 0.3188 W/m·K, as the fifth and running again (about 1 minute) gives the last row of the step-2 table. The Rint interval closes to 5.4×10⁻⁸ – 1.4×10⁻⁷ (×9.1 against the prior) and kp narrows to 0.93 – 1.55 (×8.7), both containing the true values (1.0×10⁻⁷, 1.2).

Step 5 — what the interface resistance does inside the structure

Without interface resistance (left), heat passes through the more conductive silica like stepping stones, and the flux inside the particles is large. With Rint = 1×10⁻⁷ (middle) heat is reluctant to enter the particles: the flux inside them drops by 16 % and the total flux (the conductivity) falls by 12 %, from 0.366 to 0.321 W/m·K. The graph on the right shows why. The temperature step across one voxel at a particle boundary grows from 17.5 to 23.9 mK — the added 6.4 mK is the temperature that jumps like a step at the boundary, ΔT = Rint·q (q ≈ 6×10⁴ W/m²). Less temperature difference remains across the particle (8.0 → 6.8 mK), and the particle carries less heat. Smaller particles have more interface per volume and a larger effect — the figure shows why specimens of other sizes, especially small ones, separate Rint.

Summary

  • To find the interface resistance alone, measurements of the same material at several contents suffice; more contents narrow the interval and check the model.
  • If the conductivity of the filler grade is unknown too, specimens of other particle sizes are needed: large sizes pin kp, small sizes Rint (especially its lower side). Calibration tells this before measuring with Check identifiability, and after measuring suggests which specimen to make next.
  • Read the badges as they are: Determined is a value, Upper bound/Lower bound only one limit, Not determined means the measurements do not respond to that unknown.
  • With the values put into the Case by Apply to the case, that interface resistance predicts the conductivity at other contents and particle sizes.
