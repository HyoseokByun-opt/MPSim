8. Electrical conductivity

Default: periodic finite volume (FV) · optional: NASA PuMA periodic finite elements — the equation of heat conduction, with other properties

Mathematically the problem is exactly that of heat conduction. In practice it behaves completely differently, for one reason — the property contrast is overwhelming.

8.1 How it differs from heat conduction

The thermal conductivities of epoxy and AlN differ by about 750×. A large difference, but heat also flows through the matrix, so a material performs to some extent even when the filler is not connected.

Electricity is different. Epoxy (10⁻¹⁵ S/m) and silver (10⁷ S/m) differ by 10²². At that contrast there is practically no conduction through the matrix. Therefore

The electrical conductivity is decided by whether the filler is connected from one side to the other. Connected, it is a conductor; otherwise an insulator. There is hardly anything in between. This abrupt transition is called percolation.

8.2 How it is measured

MethodDescription
Four-point probe Four probes in a line: current through the outer two, voltage across the inner two. It removes the contact resistance and is the standard method.
Two-terminal measurement Electrodes on both ends of the specimen measure the resistance. Simple, but the contact resistance is included; enough for high-resistance specimens.
Sheet resistance (Ω/sq) Films are characterised by their sheet resistance, not divided by the thickness.

The calculation is equivalent to applying 1 V across the specimen and finding the current, so it corresponds to the volume conductivity obtained with a four-point probe.

8.3 Reading the numbers

ItemMeaning and use
σ tensor (S/m)Effective electrical conductivity.
Resistivity (Ω·m)The reciprocal of σ.
Sheet resistance (Ω/sq)At the given layer thickness — the specification value of conductive and shielding films.
Connected high-value phase (x / y / z) The most important line. ● means the conductive phase is connected in that direction, ○ that it is broken.

Reading the example

Example C holds as much as 30 vol% carbonyl iron and still gives σ = 2.4×10⁻¹³ S/m — practically an insulator. That much filler, and no electricity passes.

The reason is that the particles do not touch. Spheres kept apart do not form a network at about 30 vol%; the percolation check reports "0 connected directions". Example C2, with 35 vol% of the same iron and the particles allowed to touch, gives σ = 3.2×10⁵ S/m — more than 18 orders of magnitude apart.

If a conductive filler does not raise σ, the content may not be the problem. Look at the Connected line first. With ○, the value does not rise however much filler is added, until a connection forms. Real materials behave the same way, which is why making the particles touch is the key step in producing conductive pastes.

8.5 The current-density field — where it flows

Choosing Current density |J| as the displayed quantity shows the paths the current actually takes. The values span a very wide range, so Log must be on to reveal the structure.

When connected, the current concentrates in the narrow necks where particles touch. In a real device that is where heating and degradation begin, so the maximum and its location are worth looking at together.

8.6 Assumptions and limits

AssumptionWhere reality may differ
No contact resistance Touching particles conduct perfectly. In reality oxide films and interface resistances at the contacts are large — the main reason why calculated values come out higher than measured ones.
No tunnelling Electrons crossing between particles that are very close but do not touch (a few nm) are not modelled. In CNT composites this effect does create conduction, and the calculation may underestimate it.
Connectivity on the voxel grid A gap narrower than a voxel is drawn as a contact. The default placement keeps particles apart as far as the content allows (5.5), and the default FV passes current only through shared faces. Structures with contacts are sensitive to the resolution; look at the resolution check as well — σ of example C2 changed by 16 % on a 1.5× finer grid.
Direct current No frequency dependence. High-frequency behaviour belongs to the permittivity and EMI analyses.

In short, this analysis is for deciding "is it connected, and if so, at roughly what level". Use it for percolation and for comparing conditions rather than for absolute values.

8.7 Reading the renderings — percolation made visible

The electrical analysis keeps the potential V and the current density J. For conductive fillers this picture shows the percolation itself. Below are the two epoxy + carbonyl iron examples on the same logarithmic colour scale.

What is seenMeaning
C: every particle at a similarly low J (about 10⁻⁸ A/m²) The particles are apart and the current must cross the matrix; the insulating matrix decides the result.
C2: clusters of red particles (10¹¹ A/m²) and blue ones The red clusters form a connected conducting path (spanning cluster) from top to bottom; the blue particles are isolated, not attached to it. Current flows only along the red path.
C2's arrowsThe direction of the current along the path; it turns at narrow contacts.
How to read it — when σ is close to that of the matrix there should be no red path in the picture, and when σ jumps there must be a connected red path. A path hanging on one or two contacts makes the result sensitive to the arrangement (6.4); confirm with realisations or a larger RVE. The bottleneck contacts of a path are where oxide films and contact resistance act first in the real material.
