1. What the program calculates

In one sentence: it builds the inside of a material in the computer and solves the real physical equations in it to obtain the material's properties.

New in v4
  • Interfacial thermal resistance — the filler–matrix Rint and the filler–filler contact Rc are placed directly on the voxel faces, verified against the analytical solution of Hasselman–Johnson (section 5.7, chapters 7 and 31).
  • More shapes — cube, cuboid, rounded box (superellipsoid), polyhedron (regular and irregular grains) and helix (coiled fibre); every shape supports rotation, orientation, size distributions and shells (section 5.1).
  • Pores are a material — a phase of any shape whose material is Air is a pore. A network can also be a solid, and the matrix itself can be Air (section 5.2).
  • Dense packing — when the target content is not reached, rigid clump relaxation (the multi-sphere method of DEM) moves and rotates the particles until it is. Particles are moved, never trimmed, and kept apart wherever the loading allows (section 5.5).
  • A more accurate method — thermal and electrical conduction, permittivity and permeability are solved once with a periodic finite-volume (FV) method that came 2–3× closer to known answers. The early stop of PuMA's FE iteration on large grids was fixed (section 7.2).
  • Multi-core solving — one PuMA FE solve uses several cores (about 4× on 14 threads). How the cores are split between independent solves and the threads inside each solve is chosen automatically, by whichever finishes first. A 64³ thermal + dielectric + thermal-expansion run went from 708 s to 120 s (4 cores) and 73 s (12 cores) (section 3.7).
  • TIFF export — structure.tif with a different grey value per material, a material ID map and a legend CSV. Appendix C.3 describes how to compare with another analysis program.
  • Default grid limit 500³ (section 6.7), default cores = ¼ of the PC (section 3.7).

1.1 Why this is needed

Consider a heat-spreading material made of epoxy with 40 vol% AlN filler. What is its thermal conductivity? To answer by experiment, the material must actually be mixed, cured, machined into specimens and measured — days for a single condition. Yet the questions that matter in practice are usually these:

  • How much better does it get at 45 vol% filler?
  • At the same content, are 3 µm or 10 µm particles better?
  • When the thermal conductivity rises, how much does the thermal expansion fall?
  • How much is lost when the filler agglomerates?

With dozens of combinations, not all of them can be made. This program builds the structures in the calculation, answers first, and leaves the experiments for the few promising candidates.

1.2 How it differs from a rule of mixtures

Rules of mixtures ("40 % filler, so the conductivity is about this") and approximations such as the Maxwell model are widely used. They are simple and fast.

But these formulas do not know the structure. Whether the particles are round or platelets, lie in one direction, touch to form paths or cluster changes the real property a great deal, and a formula has no place for that information. The higher the content and the more anisotropic the filler, the further the approximation drifts from reality.

This program uses no approximation: it builds the structure and solves the equations. It also shows the approximations and the theoretical bounds alongside, so that every result can be checked for physical sense (chapter 31).

1.3 The RVE — the unit of calculation

A whole material cannot be calculated. Instead, one small piece that represents the material is built and calculated. This piece is called the RVE (representative volume element).

An RVE has to satisfy two conditions at once.

  • Large enough — with only a few particles the value swings with whatever arrangement happened to be drawn.
  • Small enough — calculation time and memory grow steeply with size.

This balance is at the heart of every analysis, so the program checks automatically whether the RVE is adequate and reports passed, warning or failed (chapters 6 and 31).

1.4 What homogenisation means

Inside the RVE the temperature differs from place to place, and the heat flux in the filler differs from that in the matrix. The answer we want, however, is "one thermal conductivity for this material". Finding the value that makes a material that is non-uniform inside look like one uniform material from outside is called homogenisation.

The idea is that of the experiment. A gradient is applied from one side to the other (temperature, voltage or pressure difference), what flows is measured (heat flux, current, flow rate), and the property is defined by their ratio — what is done to a specimen in the laboratory is done inside the calculation.

The results of this program are therefore directly comparable with measured values. The calculation, however, contains none of the defects of a real specimen (interface debonding, voids, agglomerates, contact resistance of the instrument), so a calculated value usually comes out slightly better than the measured one. It is far more accurate for comparing conditions than for matching absolute values.

2. Installation and a first analysis

2.1 Installation

There are two ways to install. On a PC without internet access, such as one on a secured network, the whole package is copied and installed — conda (Miniconda/Anaconda) is not needed. On a PC with internet access the environment can also be created with conda.

PC with internet1_INSTALL.bat conda. It creates an environment in env\ from conda-forge (about 2–3 GB).

Offline installation (no conda)

  1. Copy the whole folder to the PC, on a short path (for example C:\MPSim\). Some packages create deep folders; on a long path the installation stops at the 260-character path limit of Windows with "file name too long" (WinError 206). The installer warns beforehand when the path is long.
  2. 1_INSTALL_OFFLINE.bat — uses no internet at all. It does three things:
    • unpacks the official Python 3.11 runtime of runtime\ (the embeddable distribution from python.org) into a python\ folder, registering nothing in Windows;
    • installs the 76 packages of wheels\ (NASA PuMA included) into it;
    • checks that every component imports and runs the short self-test (38 checks).
  3. 2_RUN_LOCAL.bat — starts the program; the browser opens by itself.
Installing means creating one python\ folder beside the program (about 1.6 GB), nothing more. Deleting that folder undoes it completely. The launchers use python\ when it exists and look for a conda environment only when it does not.

Why NASA PuMA works without conda

PuMA is distributed only on conda-forge, but what the conda package actually installs is an ordinary Python package (pumapy). Its four compiled modules need only python311.dll, VCRUNTIME140.dll (part of every python.org Python) and the Windows C runtime; they rely on no other library of the conda environment. tools\build_offline.py therefore packs the installed PuMA into a standard wheel (pumapy-3.2.2-cp311-cp311-win_amd64.whl), which the offline PC installs like any other package. PuMA is distributed under the NASA Open Source Agreement 1.3, and its licence file travels inside the wheel.

Verification — the package was installed with conda removed from the PATH (only the Windows folders left). The short self-test (38 checks) and three complete pipelines (filled composite, thin film, porous body) all passed, with the same numbers as in the conda environment (for example k = 0.4015 W/m·K).

Building the package again (PC with internet)

conda run -p .\env python tools\build_offline.py

It downloads the Python runtime, builds the wheel from the PuMA in that PC's conda environment and downloads every other package as a wheel for Windows and Python 3.11. Then the whole folder is moved to the offline PC.

Serving other PCs

Started with 3_RUN_SERVER.bat, the program can be opened in a browser from other PCs on the same network. The server PC does the solving, so one powerful PC serving several users is the efficient arrangement. If other PCs cannot connect, 4_NETWORK_CHECK.bat tells whether a firewall or wireless isolation is the cause.

Always start the program with the .bat files

The numbered .bat files decide, through _common.bat, which Python to use (python\ → env\ → a conda environment) and set the console encoding to UTF-8. Always start the program with them.

2.2 A first analysis, step by step

The quickest start is a preset. Following the steps below gives a first result within five minutes.

① Choose a preset

The left panel of the Home tab holds typical problems. Clicking, for example, EMC · epoxy + fused silica 50 vol% fills in the materials, the structure and the analyses at once. Under each preset the analyses it switches on are listed.

② Build the structure

In the Structure tab, click Generate structure. Nothing is solved: only the structure is built and shown in 3D, so that the filler content and the sizes can be checked by eye first. The structure is kept in that run, and when the analyses are run in step ⑤ it is used as it is instead of being generated again (section 2.4).

③ Set the resolution

The Domain tab shows the voxel size and the RVE size. The automatic setting is fine to begin with. When a value is changed, the expected grid appears at once below, with a warning if a criterion is not met (chapter 6).

④ Choose the analyses

Switch on what is needed in the Simulation and Structure Analysis tabs. Several can be on at once. The ribbon icons and the switches on the cards in the left panel both toggle them.

⑤ Run

Click Run at the top right. The progress scrolls in the Console below, and intermediate results appear as they come. When the run finishes, the Result Viewer opens by itself.

2.4 Continuing with the same structure

A run keeps the structure it built in its structure\ folder (the voxel map and the particle list). When a new analysis starts, the program looks among the runs of the same work folder and case for the same structure (same shapes, sizes, contents, grid, contact handling and seed) and, if there is one, proceeds as follows.

SituationWhat happens
Run after Generate structure The structure-only run continues as an analysis run. The structure is not generated again, and no extra run is created.
Analyses added to a finished run (e.g. thermal → thermal + dielectric) That run continues. The analyses already there are kept and only the new ones are solved, merged into the results, the report and the 3D fields. The folder name follows the analyses it holds (thermal → dielectric+thermal).
Nothing newThe program says that everything is already there and solves nothing.
Material properties or analysis options changed The results would differ, so a new run is created — but it takes over the structure of the earlier run. The earlier results are kept.
Shapes, contents, grid or seed changedA different structure, generated anew.

A run open on screen that matches takes precedence. To turn this behaviour off, clear Reuse the structure of a matching run in the Simulation tab.

3.7 Machine settings — parallel analyses and cores

The Machine section on the left of the Home tab decides how the computing resources of the PC are shared.

SettingMeaning
Analyses at once The number of analyses (jobs) run at the same time. The others wait in the queue.
Cores each The cores one analysis may use. 0 = automatic: a quarter of the PC, shared so that several analyses together never exceed the cores.

More cores for one solve — measured

PuMA's FE solve originally uses a single core: the grid's numbering tables are built in Python loops, and scipy's MINRES and NumPy's matrix products are single-threaded (measured on one 112³ thermal solve: 5.1 s for the tables, 3.4 s of MINRES vector work, 2.0 s of matrix products). v4 keeps PuMA's own equations (element matrices, periodic DOF numbering, loads, MINRES) and runs those three parts on all cores. Solved to the same tolerance, the result equals PuMA's original path within 1e-7, which the self-test checks every time (chapter 31).

The FE solve becomes about 4× faster on 14 threads and gains slowly from about 8 threads on, held back by the memory bandwidth. FV, the default for conduction, needs few iterations thanks to its FFT preconditioner (39) and takes 1.4 s on one thread — 20× faster than FE.

Splitting the cores — independent solves and threads inside a solve

Even within one analysis, every property, direction and elastic load is a separate solve. Thermal + dielectric + thermal expansion in x, y and z are 3 + 3 + 7 = 13 independent solves. The cores can be shared in two ways — several solves side by side, or several threads inside one solve. Side by side does more work per core (one elastic load: 36 core-seconds on 1 thread, 55 on 4), but when there are only a few long solves, such as elastic loads, threads finish sooner.

When an analysis starts, every possible split is evaluated and the one that finishes first is chosen. For each split the solves are assigned longest first and the finishing time is computed, with the speed-up of one solve given by S(t) = 1 / (0.15 + 0.85/t), fitted to the measurements above. The number of solves side by side is also limited by the free memory (Simulation tab, Independent solves, on by default).

Tips.
• One analysis fast — raise Cores each. Both the solves side by side and the threads inside them increase.
• Many small DOE cases — raising Analyses at once is better; structure generation and post-processing overlap as well.

A changed setting applies from the next analysis that starts; running analyses are not touched.
When the set number of simultaneous runs is reached, the Run analyses button is greyed out and does not respond. Hovering over it shows why. When several people share one server through 3_RUN_SERVER.bat, the analyses of others count against this limit too — they occupy a slot even though they do not appear in your list, so a greyed-out button means waiting.

Memory is the other constraint. Running analyses in parallel multiplies the memory as well — three analyses that need 16 GB each need 48 GB together. The Domain tab tells how much one analysis needs (section 6.7).

The queue therefore also watches the memory before it starts the next analysis. To the memory estimate of the Domain tab it adds a measured margin (×1.4 + 0.3 GB); when the total with the analyses already running would exceed 70 % of the PC's memory, or the memory free right now is not enough, it waits before starting, even with a parallel slot free — once memory spills into the disk swap, every analysis slows down by an order of magnitude. A job running alone is never held back by this, so a large analysis cannot be blocked for ever.

The queue survives a server restart. Analyses that had not started are restored as queued after a restart and run in turn. Analyses interrupted mid-way are marked failed; DOE cases can be run again with the Re-run button of section 28.4.

4. Material library (Material)

This tab is the material library. The properties of any material — matrix, filler or pore — are edited here, and new materials are added here. Which region uses which material is chosen in the Structure tab (chapter 5).

4.1 Matrix and phases

The program sees a structure as one matrix + several phases.

  • Matrix — the continuous material everything sits in: epoxy, silicone, alumina and the like.
  • Phase — a filler or a pore inside it. Any number can be added, each with its shape, size and content.

Both pick their material from the library the same way, in the Structure tab. Clicking the matrix row of the list opens the matrix editor, clicking a phase row opens the phase editor — the tab does not change. To change properties, click Edit this material in the editor or come to this tab.

Pores are phases too: choose air or vacuum as the material. The program recognises a material with an elastic modulus of 0 or less as void and treats it as pore space in the flow and diffusion analyses.

A Young's modulus of 0 means "pore". The program decides whether a region is empty space by E alone — a region with E ≤ 0 is treated as a pore, not a solid. That is how Air / pore (void) and Vacuum are defined in the library.

This turns a common shortcut into a trap: "no mechanics this time, so leave E at 0". A filler with E = 0 silently becomes a pore — the thermal conductivity is computed with air in its place, and the permeability analysis treats those particles as flow channels. Give every solid a non-zero E even when mechanics is not used (it need not be accurate).

Conversely, the flow and pore analyses (permeability, tortuosity, filtration, acoustics, porosimetry, pore network) need at least one pore region; without one they are refused with "require a pore (void) phase".
Which properties matter — only those used by the analyses switched on affect the results. For thermal conductivity alone, εr and α can be left as they are (E is the exception above). The density, however, converts weight fractions (wt%) into volume fractions and must be right when contents are entered by weight.

4.4 Anisotropic materials

Some materials — h-BN, graphite, carbon fibre — are themselves direction-dependent. The library holds them as orientation-averaged values (their names say "isotropic approximation" or "orientation-averaged").

The anisotropy that comes from aligned particles is computed (particles laid in one direction give an anisotropic result tensor), but the crystal anisotropy inside a particle is averaged away. Where it is very large, as between the in-plane and through-plane conductivity of BN platelets, this can lead to underestimates; read such results conservatively.

5.6 Coated and hollow particles (Shell)

Coating or hollow shell puts a shell on any shape. The shell is the region between the particle surface and that surface moved outwards along its normal by the thickness (an exact offset for spheres, cylinders, boxes, polyhedra and helices).

  • Surface treatment, coating — a silane layer, a metal plating.
  • Hollow particles — with Air as the phase material and SiO₂ as the shell, the particle is hollow.
The dimensions entered are those of the core inside the shell. The shell is added outside, so the outer diameter is d + 2 × thickness (a polyhedron moves every face out by the thickness). Catalogues usually give the outer diameter of hollow glass spheres: for 20 µm outside with a 1 µm wall, enter d = 18 µm. The content refers to the whole particle, shell included. With a size distribution, Wall scales with particle size keeps wall thickness over radius the same for every particle (hollow spheres of one density grade).

Thin shells demand resolution. The automatic grid sets the voxel size so that the shell thickness holds 2 voxels (conduction analyses), or 4 with elasticity and thermal expansion.

A voxelised surface is a staircase with more area than the real surface (about 1.5× for a sphere), so the program corrects R against the analytical surface area. v3 approximated the resistance by lowering the conductivity of one layer of matrix, which also blocked the heat flowing along that layer; v4 replaced it.

The critical radius. A spherical filler of radius below ac = Rint × kmatrix cannot raise the conductivity of the composite, however conductive it is. For epoxy (0.2 W/m·K) and Rint = 1×10⁻⁷ m²K/W, ac = 0.02 µm; for 1×10⁻⁶, 0.2 µm. The editor shows the critical radius of the current value. 6. Resolution and RVE size (Domain)

Most of the confidence in a result is decided in this tab: how large a piece is calculated, and how finely it is divided. It also decides whether the piece is a bulk repeated in every direction or a film of a given thickness.

6.1 What a voxel is

The calculation divides the structure into small cubes (voxels) — the three-dimensional counterpart of the pixels of a photograph. Each voxel is one cell of the computational grid, so

  • the smaller the voxels, the more exact the shapes and the more precise the results;
  • but the more of them there are: halving the voxel size makes eight times as many, and the time and memory grow by more than that.

The aim is therefore "small enough, but no smaller than needed".

6.2 RVE length and voxel size — the grid follows

In practice two things are set: the edge length of the RVE and the voxel size. The grid follows from them and is not entered separately.

6.4 Why 7× for Standard and 10× for Accurate

There is a widely used definition of "a size that counts as an RVE". Kanit et al. (2003, Int. J. Solids Struct. 40, 3647) define the RVE by the error accepted. When n RVEs of the same size V are built with different arrangements and calculated, the results scatter, and from their standard deviation D(V) the 95 % error of the mean is

ε = 2·D(V) / (Z̄·√n) ,   D²(V) ∝ V−α

— and when the size is too small, not only does the result scatter, the mean itself is biased. The criterion is therefore not a number of "times" but the size at which scatter and bias fall within the accepted error. It was measured directly with this program's generator and solvers — monodisperse spheres, periodic boundaries, ten different arrangements per size (six for E), 8–10 voxels per diameter.

Three things follow.

  • Below 5× the mean itself is wrong. No number of repetitions removes a bias. This is why Fast (4×) is for trends.
  • Standard (7×) stays within ±1 % with a single arrangement at ordinary contents of about 30 vol%.
  • At high contents and large contrasts (50 vol%, contrast 150) arrangements still scatter by ±4 % at 7× — the gaps and contacts between particles decide the conduction paths. The false anisotropy of a finite box shrinks for the same reason. For an isotropic structure, the spread of the three directional values, (max − min)/mean, was 4.3 % at 7× and 1.9 % at 10× at 50 vol%. A directional difference of this size in an isotropic material is a trace of the RVE size, not anisotropy.

Particle arrangements are random, so the same conditions give slightly different results with a different arrangement. With Realisations at 2 or more, the analysis is repeated on different arrangements and the mean is shown with its 95 % confidence interval.

With the limit raised or removed, memory, not the grid, becomes the real constraint. The check list therefore shows the Estimated peak memory, counted from the arrays every solver allocates: this much free RAM is needed.

It is judged against the memory of that PC — more than half of the installed RAM is a warning, more than 90 % a failure, but nothing is refused. It is an estimate, not a measurement; keep a margin.

When several analyses run at once, multiply by their number. Three 16 GB analyses in parallel need 48 GB. 7. Thermal conductivity

Default: periodic finite volume (FV), solved once · optional: NASA PuMA periodic finite elements (FE), comparison of the two

The quantity most looked at for heat-spreading materials. This chapter explains not just k but every number that appears in this section of the Result Viewer.

7.1 First, how thermal conductivity is measured

A simulated value is easiest to understand once it is clear what the experiment measures. The laboratory obtains the thermal conductivity in two ways.

(1) Measured directly — steady-state methods

A temperature difference across the specimen drives a constant flow of heat, which is measured, and Fourier's law

q = −k · (ΔT / L)   →   k = q · L / ΔT

gives k. The guarded hot plate (ASTM C177) and ASTM D5470, common for TIMs, belong here. The difficulty is the contact resistance: the fine gaps between instrument and specimen add their resistance to the specimen's own, so the specimen is measured at several thicknesses and its resistance is taken from the slope.

(2) Obtained indirectly — transient methods (laser flash)

The more common route in practice. The front of the specimen is heated by a short pulse and the rate at which the back warms up gives the thermal diffusivity α (ASTM E1461). The conductivity is not measured but calculated as

k = α · ρ · cp

so obtaining k by laser flash requires the density ρ and the specific heat cp separately. The Thermal diffusivity and Volumetric heat capacity described below correspond to this relation.

In short — steady-state methods measure k directly, transient methods measure α and calculate k. The simulator solves a steady-state problem for k directly and shows α calculated back from k. When comparing with laser flash, compare α with α: fewer assumptions are mixed in.

7.2 How the simulator calculates it

It repeats in the calculation what the experiment does.

  1. Every voxel gets the conductivity of its region: filler voxels the k of the filler, matrix voxels that of the matrix.
  2. A unit temperature gradient is applied in one direction (for example ΔT = 1 K along x), and the steady heat equation ∇·(k∇T) = 0 is solved, one voxel per computational cell.
  3. The solution gives the heat flux q in every voxel. The ratio of the volume average of q to the applied mean gradient is the effective conductivity in that direction.
  4. Repeating for x, y and z fills the 3×3 tensor.

Boundary conditions: periodic

An experiment cuts a specimen and holds its two faces; the calculation joins the opposite faces of the RVE — it looks at one piece of an endlessly repeated material. This removes the artificial effect of cut faces, and even a small RVE converges quickly to the value inside the material. PuMA's own finite-volume calculation fixes the temperatures of two faces and is therefore not the default (it remains a Solver option).

Two discretisations — FV (default) and FE

The same voxel grid can be solved two ways. The periodic finite volume (FV) method keeps one temperature per voxel, and heat passes only where a voxel shares a face with its neighbour (harmonic mean of the two conductivities). NASA PuMA's periodic finite elements (FE) keep a temperature at every voxel corner; a corner on a boundary belongs to filler and matrix alike, so heat also crosses between voxels that touch only at an edge or a corner. FE therefore crosses narrow gaps and contacts easily, as if the filler were half a voxel larger, and comes out higher the coarser the grid.

Which is right was checked on problems with known answers. A simple cubic array (one sphere per periodic cell) has a small cell and can be solved with up to 256 voxels per diameter; at that resolution FE and FV extrapolate to the same value within 0.1 %. Chapter 25 explains this chart in detail. In brief — Wiener and Hashin–Shtrikman are bounds no physical material can cross, so a calculated value outside them is an error. A difference from approximate models such as Maxwell-Garnett or Bruggeman is not an error but the effect of the microstructure: the models know nothing of orientation or contacts.

k tensor (W/m·K)

xx, yy and zz are the effective conductivities for heat flowing along x, y and z. Similar values mean isotropy; one larger value means the filler is aligned, or a connected path has formed, in that direction. The Isotropic mean below is the mean of the three, to compare with a measurement that does not distinguish directions.

Thermal resistivity (m·K/W)

The reciprocal of the conductivity, for when it is easier to think in resistances (series stacks, for example).

Area-specific thermal resistance (mm²·K/W)

The value TIM specifications actually use — the thermal resistance per area of a layer of thickness L,

R″ = L / k

It takes the thickness entered in Layer thickness, so it changes with that thickness. This is what ASTM D5470 yields — except that the measured value includes contact resistance, while this value is the resistance of the material alone. That is the most common reason why the calculation comes out lower than the measurement.

Thermal diffusivity (mm²/s)

It says how fast heat spreads. By definition

α = k / (ρ · cp)

calculated from the k just solved and ρ·cp as the volume-weighted mean of the densities and specific heats of the phases. Where the conductivity says "how well heat passes in the steady state", the diffusivity says "how fast a change of temperature travels". A material can pass heat well (large k) and still respond slowly, if it stores much heat (large ρcp). It is directly comparable with α measured by laser flash.

Volumetric heat capacity (MJ/m³·K)

ρ·cp — the denominator of the relation above: the heat needed to warm a unit volume by 1 K.

Anisotropy (xx+yy)/2 : zz

The ratio of the in-plane mean to the through-thickness value. Above 1, heat flows better in-plane. Platelet fillers (h-BN, graphite) raise it strongly — a drawback when heat must cross the thickness.

Field-line tortuosity / Field lines that cross the sample

The result of actually tracing the field lines of the heat flux. The mean length of the lines that cross the specimen over the straight distance is the tortuosity; close to 1, heat flows straight. Few crossing lines mean the heat is channelled through a few paths.

How it is calculated

The resistance sits directly on the voxel faces. 8. Electrical conductivity

Default: periodic finite volume (FV) · optional: NASA PuMA periodic finite elements — the equation of heat conduction, with other properties

Mathematically the problem is exactly that of heat conduction. In practice it behaves completely differently, for one reason — the property contrast is overwhelming.

8.1 How it differs from heat conduction

The thermal conductivities of epoxy and AlN differ by about 750×. A large difference, but heat also flows through the matrix, so a material performs to some extent even when the filler is not connected.

Electricity is different. Epoxy (10⁻¹⁵ S/m) and silver (10⁷ S/m) differ by 10²². At that contrast there is practically no conduction through the matrix. Therefore

The electrical conductivity is decided by whether the filler is connected from one side to the other. Connected, it is a conductor; otherwise an insulator. There is hardly anything in between. This abrupt transition is called percolation.

8.2 How it is measured

MethodDescription
Four-point probe Four probes in a line: current through the outer two, voltage across the inner two. It removes the contact resistance and is the standard method.
Two-terminal measurement Electrodes on both ends of the specimen measure the resistance. Simple, but the contact resistance is included; enough for high-resistance specimens.
Sheet resistance (Ω/sq) Films are characterised by their sheet resistance, not divided by the thickness.

The calculation is equivalent to applying 1 V across the specimen and finding the current, so it corresponds to the volume conductivity obtained with a four-point probe.

9. Permittivity and loss

Default: periodic finite volume (FV) · optional: NASA PuMA periodic finite elements — the equation of heat conduction, with the permittivity as the property

The values that decide how much a signal slows down and how much of it is lost when this material is an insulating layer — basic properties of package substrates, antenna substrates and capacitor design.

9.1 Why it matters

What Dk doesWhat Df does
Sets the propagation speed of a signal. The delay grows with √Dk, so a high Dk slows signals — why high-speed substrates look for a low Dk. The fraction of the signal lost as heat. The insertion loss grows roughly with Df·√Dk·f, so at high frequencies Df decides.

9.3 How the simulator calculates it

Exactly like the heat conduction of chapter 7, with the potential in place of the temperature and the permittivity in place of the conductivity.

  1. A potential difference is applied across the RVE; the other faces are periodic.
  2. The potential and the electric displacement inside are solved.
  3. The mean displacement gives the effective permittivity.
  4. Three directions give the tensor.

How the loss (Df) comes out — not a simple average

The loss of a composite is not the volume average of the tanδ of its phases. It is decided by where the energy of the electric field actually resides. Less field enters a phase of high permittivity, so its loss counts for less than its volume fraction.

The panel therefore says Dissipation factor Df (energy-weighted) — the loss weighted by the field energy. This is possible because the solved field gives the field energy in each phase exactly.

11.2 How shielding happens — three mechanisms

SEtotal = R + A + M　(dB)
MechanismPrincipleGrows with
Reflection (R) When the impedances of material and air differ, the wave bounces off the surface and does not even enter. Higher conductivity. Dominant at low frequencies.
Absorption (A) The wave that enters decays as it turns into heat. Greater thickness and larger σ·μ. Dominant at high frequencies.
Multiple reflections (M) Bouncing back and forth inside, part of the wave comes back out. It can be negative (it reduces the shielding). Matters only in thin plates; vanishes in thick ones.
The difference between reflection and absorption matters in practice. A wave stopped by reflection goes elsewhere and causes problems there. Materials that stop waves by absorption are therefore preferred, and that needs magnetism (μr), not just conductivity — the reason for magnetic fillers such as carbonyl iron.

11.3 How the simulator calculates it

In two steps. First the microstructure (RVE) is solved for the effective properties of the composite; then a plane wave meeting a plate of thickness t with those properties at normal incidence is transmitted and reflected according to the exact formulae (transmission line, Schelkunoff). Since v4 the first step is done at every frequency separately.

Complex homogenisation per frequency (default)

  1. Every material gets its complex admittivity at the frequency f: κ = σ + jωε₀εr(1 − j tanδ) — conduction and displacement current together.
  2. The RVE is solved directly with these values (the finite-volume method of the conduction analysis in complex numbers, in the two in-plane directions x and y). The real part of the result κeff(f) is the effective conductivity σ′(f), the imaginary part the effective permittivity ε′(f).
  3. This is repeated at 10 frequencies between fmin and fmax (Solved frequencies) and interpolated logarithmically in between. The permeability μeff is the static value of the magnetic analysis.
  4. σ′(f), ε′(f) and μeff give the reflection, absorption and multiple reflections of the plate.

The static method (up to v3, selectable under Method) solved the DC σ and the static ε separately and combined them, missing the effects where the two currents mix.

  • Metal particles kept apart — at radio frequencies metal particles behave as perfect conductors and raise the permittivity of the composite. A static permittivity analysis uses the metal's εr (usually 1) as it is and comes out lower instead.
  • Weakly conductive fillers (carbon, semiconducting fillers) — charge piling up at the interfaces and relaxing, the interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner) polarisation, makes ε′ and σ′ change with frequency. The static method has no such change.
  • A connected conductive network — the conduction current dominates and both methods give the same value.
Every frequency solves two in-plane directions in complex numbers, about 20 conduction solves more in all. With a connected network, ε′ is below a millionth of σ′ and is not resolved ("—" in the table); it does not affect the shielding.
The shielding is right only if the results of chapters 8–10 are right. The electrical conductivity in particular is decided by percolation (chapter 8): if the filler is not connected, the shielding comes out close to zero as well.

  • the dynamic range of measuring equipment usually ends at 100–120 dB, and
  • real enclosures leak far more through seams and apertures than through the material.
Read above 80 dB as "sufficient as a material" and use the digits beyond only for comparison. The real performance is decided by design and assembly.

The Validity of the results gives the frequency where this breaks down — for the carbonyl iron of example C2 (σ 107 S/m, μr 50) the skin depth falls below the particle size from a few hundred MHz on.
Infinite flat plate No edges, curvature, apertures or seams — where most real shielding failures happen.
Plane wave, normal incidence A plane wave from afar is assumed. The near field (right beside an antenna, say) behaves differently.

In short, the result is for comparing candidate materials and judging their level. It cannot replace certified values; the final judgement belongs to an ASTM D4935 measurement.

10. Magnetic permeability

Default: periodic finite volume (FV) · optional: NASA PuMA periodic finite elements — the same equation for the magnetic scalar potential

10.1 How it is measured

MethodDescription

The equation of heat conduction (chapter 7), with the magnetic scalar potential in place of the temperature and the permeability in place of the conductivity.

  1. A magnetic potential difference is applied across the RVE; the other faces are periodic.
  2. The flux density inside is solved.
  3. The mean flux gives the effective permeability; three directions give the tensor.

10.4 The most important fact — far below the filler

The reason: the flux must cross the epoxy. A magnetic circuit is like an electric one: the worst section in series dominates the whole. The thin resin layers between particles are a large magnetic resistance (an air gap) and pull the overall permeability down, however good the filler. This is the demagnetisation effect.

For real magnetic composites, shape and connectivity therefore matter more than the filler's own performance. Flat flakes laid in the plane reduce the gaps the flux has to cross and raise the permeability strongly — why noise-suppression sheets use flat magnetic powders, not spheres. Switching to a Spheroid (c/a < 1) at the same content in the Structure tab shows the effect directly.

The bounds support this. The Wiener upper bound of 18.2 is the value if the filler were perfectly connected; the lower bound is 1.52. The calculated 4.896 lies between them, far below the upper bound — above Maxwell-Garnett (2.48), which treats the particles as apart, and below Bruggeman (5.94), which weights their connection heavily. At the same content, only better connectivity can raise it.

10.5 Why the directions differ

This example lets the particles overlap (Overlap permitted in the Structure tab; 66,340 voxel pairs of different particles touch on the grid, 11,103 of them across a face). When touching particles join into chains, the directional values depend strongly on where the chains run. The permeability in x is 7–9 % below y and z (a warning in the checks), and the electrical conductivity differs by 46 % between directions, recorded as failed (chapters 8 and 31).

When a structure that should be isotropic scatters between directions, the RVE holds too few particles. Read this example's μr as "about 4.6–5.1"; for a value to report, enlarge the RVE or raise Realisations to obtain a mean with its confidence interval (chapter 31). The resolution is not the cause — the resolution check changed by less than 1 %.

10.6 Assumptions and limits

AssumptionWhere reality may differ
Linear — no saturation The largest limitation. Magnetic materials saturate in strong fields and lose permeability. This calculation covers the small-signal (initial permeability) range only; the behaviour of an inductor at its rated current is out of reach.
No hysteresis The history of the B–H curve and its loss (μ″) are not computed; no core-loss assessment.
DC, static No frequency dependence. The permeability of real magnetic fillers falls sharply above a few hundred MHz (Snoek limit) — the main reason the high-frequency shielding of chapter 11 is overestimated.

10.7 Reading the renderings — the gaps decide the permeability

The permeability analysis keeps the magnetic potential and the local field enhancement |H|/H₀. Below is example C2 (epoxy + carbonyl iron 35 vol%).

  • Inside the high-permeability particles H is almost 0 — the flux B continues, but H = B/μ becomes small.
  • Instead H crowds into the matrix gaps between particles. The flux meets a large "magnetic resistance" at every gap it crosses, and the gaps limit the permeability — why the composite's μeff stays at 4.9 although iron's μ is in the hundreds (demagnetisation).
  • The narrower and fewer the gaps (the more the particles line up along the field), the larger μeff. Aligning platelet or needle fillers with the field visibly closes these gaps.

12. Elasticity and CTE

Solver: FANS (the periodic Q1 voxel finite elements of NASA PuMA, solved by FFT-preconditioned conjugate gradients — default since v5) · NASA PuMA FE (optional). Six strains + one temperature load = seven load cases

Many reliability problems of semiconductor packages come from joining materials whose thermal expansions differ. This analysis obtains the elastic moduli and the thermal expansion of the composite together, and shows the stresses that arise inside when the temperature changes.

12.1 How they are measured

Coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE)

TMA (thermomechanical analysis) or a dilatometer measures the change in length while the specimen is heated slowly.

α = (1 / L) · (dL / dT)

Usually in ppm/K (= 10⁻⁶/K). The value changes strongly through the glass transition, so the temperature range always comes with it.

Elastic moduli

From the initial slope of the stress–strain curve in tension or bending (universal testing machine), or from the longitudinal and shear wave speeds measured ultrasonically. The ultrasonic method is better for the small-strain moduli.

12.2 How the simulator calculates them

Instead of pulling in one direction at a time as an experiment does, seven solves settle the whole behaviour at once.

  1. Six strains — three normal (x, y, z) and three shear strains, each of unit size, give the stresses in the RVE; each fills one column of the 6×6 stiffness matrix C*.
  2. One temperature load — with the strain held at zero, only the temperature rises by ΔT. The internal stresses from the phases trying to expand differently give the thermal expansion.

The solver of v5: FANS

Up to v4 the elastic analysis solved NASA PuMA's finite elements by MINRES iterations. On larger grids the iteration count grew with the grid, and on a 500³ grid more than 30 hours were not enough to reach a relative residual of 1e-5. v5 keeps the same elements (periodic Q1 voxel finite elements) but solves them by conjugate gradients preconditioned with the exact inverse, computed by FFT, of the stiffness of a homogeneous reference material (FANS, Leuschner and Fritzen 2018). The inverse of the reference is one 3×3 matrix per frequency, almost free, and the iteration count stays at a few tens per load whatever the grid.

A correction to the elasticity and CTE results up to v4. During the development of v5, the isotropic-material function of PuMA 3.2.2 was found to double the shear modulus of every phase (it uses engineering shear strains while placing 2μ on the stiffness diagonal) and to return the shear axes in reversed order. v5 builds the material matrices itself and corrects the axes. In an epoxy + Al₂O₃ 25 vol% example the v4 values were biased by C11 +12 %, bulk modulus +11 % and thermal expansion −8 %. Levin's theorem checks only that C and α agree with each other and could not catch this; v5 checks the G of a homogeneous RVE and the Hashin–Shtrikman shear bounds separately. The stresses that actually arise inside when the temperature changes by ΔT, per phase. ΔT is set on screen, usually the cooling from the process temperature to room temperature.

α tensor (ppm/K)

Six components (xx, yy, zz, yz, xz, xy). The first three are the linear expansions in each direction, similar when isotropic. Example A gives 21.7 ppm/K (xx 22.0, yy 21.5, zz 21.7). Epoxy alone is usually around 60 ppm/K, so 40 vol% AlN lowers it to a third — reducing the CTE is one of the main reasons for adding filler. The value agrees with the exact two-phase relation (Levin's theorem) within one part per million.

E, G, ν (per direction)

Young's modulus E (stiffness in tension), shear modulus G and Poisson's ratio ν per direction; anisotropic materials differ between directions. νxy and νyx may differ — not an error but a normal property of anisotropic materials.

Voigt / Reuss / Hill

Three ways to summarise an anisotropic result by one isotropic value: Voigt the upper bound, Reuss the lower, Hill their mean. Use Hill for rough comparison with other data or when one number must be reported.

Universal anisotropy index AU

0 is perfect isotropy; larger is more directional. Spherical fillers placed at random should give close to 0; a large value signals a small RVE or biased particles.

Much stiff filler gives AU of 0.1–0.2 even with random spheres. Example A (AlN 40 vol%) gives 0.14: the normal stiffness along the grid axes exceeds that at 45° (Zener ratio 0.71). It appears to come from voxelised particles and thin matrix gaps lying parallel to the grid axes, not from the real material, and shrinks at lower contents or on finer grids. To pass such a material on as one isotropic value, use the Hill mean; do not read the directional differences of E and G as real anisotropy.

Stiffness C* / Compliance S*

The full 6×6 matrices, in a form that can be entered directly as this material's properties in other programs (structural FEA), linking the scales of a multiscale analysis.

13. Viscosity and flowability

Solvers: the built-in FANS voxel finite elements (creeping flow, B-bar elements) · Krieger–Dougherty · Farr–Groot maximum packing · Chateau–Ovarlez–Trung non-Newtonian homogenisation

Before cure, underfills (MUF), moulding compounds (EMC), adhesives and pastes are liquids. This analysis finds how much the filler raises the viscosity, how fast the compound fills a narrow gap, and whether the filler settles. The more filler is added to improve the thermal and mechanical properties, the worse the flow — the point of this analysis is to see both on the same structure.

13.1 What it calculates

The viscosity of the filled liquid divided by that of the resin (matrix) alone, the relative viscosity μr.

μr = μcompound / μresin

μr is set by the amount, shape, size distribution and arrangement of the filler alone, independent of the resin's viscosity. Once known it can be multiplied in when the temperature changes the resin viscosity. With little filler it rises slowly, as Einstein's 1 + 2.5φ; close to the maximum packing fraction φm, where the particles can no longer move, it diverges. Choosing a filler content in practice is mostly a question of how far from this divergence one stays.

13.2 How it is measured

MethodDescription
Rotational rheometer
(parallel plate, cone and plate)		 The sample between two plates is sheared and the torque measured; varying the shear rate gives the flow curve (viscosity versus shear rate). Divided by the resin alone it gives μr — the measurement most directly comparable with this analysis.
Capillary viscometer Pushes the sample through a thin tube and measures the pressure drop; suited to high shear rates (moulding, dispensing).
Spiral flow
(EMC)		 The compound is pushed into a spiral mould and the length it flows before curing is measured — a practical index combining viscosity and cure rate.
Underfill flow test The time an underfill needs to fill a given distance in a constant gap between two glass plates; compared with the Washburn time of 13.4.

13.3 How the simulator calculates it

The flow solved on the RVE

Slow viscous flow (creeping or Stokes flow) obeys the equations of an incompressible elastic solid, with velocity for displacement, strain rate for strain and viscosity for shear modulus. It is therefore solved with the FANS voxel finite elements of the elastic analysis. B-bar elements prevent the locking of incompressibility, and the filler is a fluid 1000× more viscous than the resin, practically rigid (the intrinsic viscosity differs by 0.4 % between 1000× and 10000× — a self-test check). A unit shear rate is applied to the whole RVE in the yz, xz and xy planes and the viscosity is obtained from the mean stress; uniaxial extension is solved once more.

Maximum packing fraction φm

  • Spherical fillers (size distributions and blends of fillers included): the Farr–Groot algorithm (J. Chem. Phys. 131, 244104, 2009) gives the random close packing directly from the particle-size distribution, by turning the packing of 3D spheres into the filling of a 1D line with rods. It reproduces the 27 3D simulations of the paper (binary size ratios 1:2 to 1:10) within 0.011 and gives 0.6435 for equal spheres.
  • Non-spherical fillers (platelets, fibres, polyhedra and others): the structure generator packs the same particles until they stop growing. Rotating particles converge very slowly, so the number of steps is limited and the value is shown as a lower estimate.
  • Friction: rough fillers, or fillers without dispersant, jam under shear before random close packing (0.585 against 0.64 for spheres, Boyer et al. 2011). Particle friction = Frictional multiplies φm by 0.585/0.64; the results show both cases.

Intrinsic viscosity [η]

2.5 for spheres (Einstein); Simha's limits for slender fibres and thin platelets. For other shapes an RVE with the same particles diluted to 4 vol% is solved separately and [η] = (μr − 1)/φ.

Which value becomes the result

When the resin films between particles are thinner than one or two voxels, the grid cannot resolve the lubrication and the RVE value comes out too low. Up to 35 vol% filler with particles not touching, the RVE flow solve is the best estimate; above, Krieger–Dougherty with φm.

μr = (1 − φ/φm)−[η] φm    (Krieger–Dougherty 1959)

Non-Newtonian resins, underfill, settling

  • For a shear-thinning resin (power law, Carreau, Cross), the flow curve of the compound follows from the homogenisation of Chateau, Ovarlez and Trung (2008): the resin between the particles is sheared A = √(μr/(1−φ)) times faster than the mean.
  • Underfill filling time: capillary flow in a parallel-plate gap (Washburn), t = 3μL² / (hγ cos θ).
  • Settling: the Stokes velocity times the hindrance of the other particles, (1 − φ)4.65 (Richardson–Zaki).

13.6 Points to watch

Bubbles (air) are calculated without surface tension. The flow solve has no surface tension, so bubbles deform freely and lower the viscosity, to 1 − (5/3)φ in the dilute limit. Small bubbles held round by surface tension (low capillary number) raise it instead, to 1 + φ (Taylor). With bubbles present the checks show this warning.
At high contents (50 vol% and above) φm is almost the whole result. At 60 vol%, changing φm from 0.64 to just 0.70 changes μr from 84 to 30, almost threefold. With one or two measured flow curves, the most reliable use — simpler than Calibration (chapter 30) — is to fit φm to the measurement, as in "φm that puts Krieger–Dougherty through the RVE value", and predict other contents with it.
  • The RVE sees only steady shear viscosity. Thixotropy, time-dependent yield stress and the viscosity rise of curing enter only through the resin model (yield stress, Carreau and so on).
  • Nano-fillers (a few hundred nm and below) raise the viscosity further through Brownian motion and adsorbed surface layers. These effects are not in the calculation, so measurements come out higher at the same content.
  • Touching particles are seen by the RVE as one rigid cluster, and the value becomes too high; Krieger–Dougherty then becomes the best estimate automatically.

14. Moisture uptake, diffusion and swelling

Package resins absorb moisture from the air. During reflow the absorbed water causes delamination and popcorn cracks by its vapour pressure, and it swells the resin, causing warpage and interface stresses. This analysis finds how much moisture a filled compound takes up, how fast, and how much it swells.

14.3 How the simulator calculates it

Every material can carry three optional properties (Moisture and glass transition in the Material tab): the moisture diffusivity Dw, the saturated uptake csat (wt %) and the hygroscopic swelling coefficient CME. A material without them is taken to absorb no moisture (inorganic fillers such as silica and alumina). The resin values of the library are typical of 85 °C/85 % RH; replace them with datasheet values or the measurements of 14.2 for a real material.

Why the water activity, not the concentration

Moisture moves by differences of the activity a = c/csat (equal to the relative humidity), not of the concentration. At an interface between two materials the concentration jumps (each takes up a different amount) while the activity is continuous. The equation of the conduction analysis is therefore solved for the activity, with the permeability P = Dw·csat as the "conductivity". The effective diffusivity of the compound is that effective permeability divided by the mean saturated concentration.

Deff = Peff / ⟨csat⟩,    P = Dw csat

A filler that absorbs no moisture becomes an impenetrable obstacle in this equation by itself. For dilute spheres Deff/Dresin = 2/(2+φ) (Maxwell), which the self-test checks.

Uptake curve and swelling

  • Uptake curve: Crank's solution for a plate of thickness L absorbing through both faces (or one). The half-saturation time is t50 = 0.0492 L²/D.
  • Swelling: at saturation every material gets an eigenstrain of CME × (its own moisture mass fraction), and the FANS solve of the thermal expansion gives the strain of the whole compound. When the same run has an elasticity/CTE analysis, its stiffness is reused and only one more solve is needed.

14.5 Properties above Tg (an option of Elasticity and CTE)

Reflow (260 °C) happens mostly above the Tg of the resin. With Also above Tg on in the elasticity/CTE analysis, materials that carry Tg properties (E above Tg, CTE above Tg) are switched to their rubbery state (ν 0.45, B-bar elements) and solved once more — the α1/α2 and the room- and high-temperature moduli of an EMC datasheet.

The resin's own α2 is 180 ppm/K, but the compound's is 95.5 ppm/K: above Tg the stiff filler holds the softened resin. The modulus falls 66×, so stresses and warpage during reflow are entirely different from those calculated with room-temperature properties.

The material values decide the result. The moisture and Tg values of the library are representative of a kind of resin; uptake and diffusivity differ several-fold between grades of the same epoxy. What this analysis gives accurately is how much the amount, shape and arrangement of the filler change those values. Enter the resin values from measurements or datasheets.

15. Permeability

Open-source solver: NASA PuMA Stokes-flow finite elements — on the connected pores only

How easily a fluid passes through a porous material. The basic property of filters, membranes, sintered metals and porous ceramics.

15.1 How it is measured

A pressure difference Δp is applied across the specimen and the flow rate Q coming out is measured. Darcy's law gives the permeability.

Q / A = (K / μ) · (Δp / L)

A is the cross-section, L the thickness and μ the viscosity of the fluid. The permeability K has the unit of an area (m²) and is a property of the structure alone, independent of the fluid. The darcy, from petroleum engineering, is also common: 1 darcy ≈ 9.87×10⁻¹³ m².

What matters in the experiment matters in the calculation too — at too high a flow rate Darcy's law breaks down. Inertia appears, the pressure loss is no longer proportional to the velocity, and the measured value is not the true permeability. The Forchheimer coefficient below tells where that limit lies.

15.2 How the simulator calculates it

  1. Only the connected pores are kept. Isolated pores cannot be entered by the fluid and are excluded. Because this distinction decides the result, the open and closed porosities are shown together.
  2. The Stokes equations are solved in the pores — slow flow dominated by viscosity, the same condition under which Darcy's law holds in the experiment.
  3. On the walls the no-slip condition: the fluid velocity is 0.
  4. The mean of the velocity field and the applied pressure gradient give K. Three directions give the tensor.

16. Filtration efficiency

Open-source basis: Lagrangian particle tracking in the Stokes velocity field solved by PuMA

How well a filter catches particles of each size. The flow field of the previous chapter is reused: particles are released one by one and followed through it.

16.1 How it is measured

Particles are counted upstream and downstream of the filter. A monodisperse aerosol (usually DEHS, PSL latex or NaCl) is passed through the filter and an optical particle counter (OPC) or condensation particle counter (CPC) measures the concentrations on both sides:

Efficiency E = 1 − (downstream count / upstream count)

Repeating this at different sizes gives the fractional efficiency curve. The HEPA classes (EN 1822) are defined by the minimum of this curve.

16.2 Why a "most penetrating size" exists

A fact that surprises at first: small particles are caught more easily. The capture mechanisms depend on size.

MechanismHow it worksSizes it catches
Diffusion (Brownian) Very small particles are knocked about by gas molecules until they touch a wall and stick. below ~0.1 µm
Interception Even following a streamline, a particle touches the wall one radius early. above ~0.5 µm
Inertial impaction Heavy or fast particles cannot follow a bending streamline, go straight on and hit. above ~1 µm

Diffusion weakens with size while interception and impaction strengthen, so somewhere in between lies the size that is caught least: the MPPS (most penetrating particle size). A filter's performance is specified at this point.

16.3 How the simulator calculates it

It does in the computer what the experiment does.

  1. The velocity field of the permeability analysis is scaled to the specified face velocity.
  2. Particles are released at the inlet, more where the flow is larger (they arrive with the air).
  3. The motion of each particle is integrated — drag by the fluid, inertia (how far it fails to follow) and the random walk of Brownian motion act together.
  4. A particle whose surface touches a wall (centre within one radius) is captured.
  5. One leaving through the opposite face counts as penetrating.
  6. Repeating over sizes gives the curve.

16.4 Reading the numbers

ItemMeaning and use
Most penetrating size The size caught least — the value that sets the filter class; 0.143 µm in example B.
Efficiency at that size The efficiency at that size; 70 % in example B, a coarse filter far from HEPA (99.97 %).
Pressure drop The pressure loss from the permeability found before — the cost paid for the efficiency.
Quality factor −ln(penetration) / pressure drop — efficiency and pressure loss in one figure, the fairest value for comparing filters. Larger is better.
Stokes number Measure of inertia. Much below 1, the particles follow the streamlines well (no impaction).
Péclet number Ratio of convection to diffusion. The smaller, the more Brownian motion dominates.
Returned to the inlet Particles that diffused back out through the inlet. They did not pass the filter and are excluded from the efficiency; the number grows at the smallest sizes.

16.5 Seeing the particle tracks

Show the particle tracks shows in 3D the paths the particles actually took. The tracks of the most penetrating size open, so one can see directly how those particles get through the structure.

The tracks of very small particles (0.01 µm and the like) are hardly visible: Brownian motion is so strong that they stick to a nearby wall right after entering — which is exactly what "caught well" means.

16.6 Assumptions and limits

AssumptionWhere reality may differ

Every touch sticks Large particles hitting fast can bounce off (rebound); then the calculation overestimates.
The structure does not change No clogging by deposited particles (loading). Only the initial performance of a clean filter is calculated; in use the efficiency and the pressure drop of a real filter both rise.
Gravity neglected Negligible in the sub-micron range that decides filter performance.
Spherical particles Fibrous or agglomerated particles behave differently.
This analysis is therefore not for judging an absolute class but for comparing how efficiency and pressure loss trade off when the structure changes. Comparing the quality factor while varying the pore size, porosity and thickness is the most useful way to use it.

16.7 Reading the rendering — where the particles are caught

The paths of the filtration analysis are the tracks of particles carried by the flow field of the permeability analysis. Large particles are intercepted at narrow throats; very small ones diffuse to a wall by Brownian motion. Sizes whose tracks end early are caught well; the size with the most tracks passing all the way through is the most penetrating particle size (MPPS) of the result graph.

17. Diffusion and tortuosity

Open-source solver: NASA PuMA continuum tortuosity — the steady diffusion equation in the pores

How much more slowly a substance (ions, gases, moisture) passes through a porous structure. A key value in the design of battery electrodes, fuel-cell gas-diffusion layers, catalyst supports and moisture barriers.

17.1 What tortuosity is

Even through open pores a substance cannot go straight. It must detour and squeeze through narrow throats, so the apparent diffusion is slower than in free space. Two things cause the slowdown:

  • Porosity ε — the cross-section open to passage is smaller to begin with.
  • Tortuosity τ — within it the path winds.
Deff / Dbulk = ε / τ
Tortuosity is defined differently from source to source. Some call the τ of the formula above the tortuosity, some τ², and some use the ratio of path lengths (Lactual/Lstraight). This program shows the τ of the formula above (tortuosity factor). Match the definition before comparing with other data — the values differ by a square.

17.2 How it is measured

MethodDescription
Diffusion cell
(Wicke–Kallenbach)		 A tracer gas flows on one side of the specimen and a carrier gas on the other; the concentration that crosses over is measured. The steady flux gives Deff directly.
Time lag A concentration is suddenly applied on one side and the time until it appears on the other is measured; the lag is set by the diffusivity and the thickness.
Conductivity method
(formation factor)		 The most widely used. The specimen is filled with an electrolyte and its resistance measured. Ionic diffusion and conduction follow the same equation, so the resistance ratio F = σelectrolyte/σspecimen equals τ/ε — the standard method for battery electrodes.
NMR, tracer methods Observe the actual displacement of molecules directly.
The reason the conductivity method works is also the basis of this analysis — diffusion, electrical conduction and heat conduction are mathematically the same equation, so the same solver solves all three.

17.3 How the simulator calculates it

  1. Only the connected pores are kept; a substance cannot enter isolated pores.
  2. The steady diffusion (Laplace) equation is solved in them, with a concentration difference across two faces and periodic side faces.
  3. On the solid walls nothing passes (zero flux).
  4. The total flux gives Deff; the formula above gives τ back.
  5. This is repeated in three directions.

17.4 Reading the numbers

Gas molecules normally spread by colliding with one another. When the pores are smaller than the mean free path (about 68 nm in air at room temperature and pressure), molecules hit the walls before each other. The diffusivity then becomes proportional to the pore size — Knudsen diffusion.

18. Acoustic absorption

How much sound a porous material absorbs, frequency by frequency. The results of earlier analyses (permeability, tortuosity, pore size) are the inputs — a typical example of several analyses combined.

18.1 How it is measured

MethodDescription

18.2 How absorption happens

Sound entering the pores moves the air back and forth through narrow passages, losing energy by friction with the walls. Compression and expansion also produce heat that escapes into the walls, so energy is lost as heat too. An absorber must therefore have open pores — closed-cell foam (polystyrene) insulates heat but absorbs almost no sound.

The flow resistivity must be moderate. Too low and the sound simply passes; too high and it cannot enter and is reflected. Real absorbers mostly lie in 5×10³ – 5×10⁴ Pa·s/m². A calculated value far outside this range means the structure cannot serve as an absorber.

19. Radiative heat transfer

Open-source solver: NASA PuMA ray casting — counts how far light travels in the pores before it is stopped

When a porous material gets hot, radiation across the pores carries a large share of the heat. It is the main reason insulators lose performance at high temperature, and this analysis calculates that share separately.

19.1 Why insulators get worse at high temperature

Radiative transfer is proportional to the fourth power of temperature; expressed as an equivalent conductivity it grows with the cube of temperature.

krad = 16 σ n² T³ / (3 β)

σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, n the refractive index and β the extinction coefficient. Negligible at room temperature, it can no longer be neglected at 500 °C and may exceed solid conduction above 1000 °C.

The formula shows that a larger β means less radiation. β is "how quickly light is blocked", so finer pores or opaque particles (opacifiers) block radiation — why TiO₂ or SiC powders are added to aerogels and insulating bricks.

19.2 How it is measured

MethodDescription
High-temperature conductivity Guarded hot plate or laser flash at rising temperatures; the part that bends up with T³ is the radiative share. Radiation is not measured directly but separated from the total.
FT-IR transmission and reflection Infrared transmittance through thin slices; the attenuation with thickness gives β directly — the measurement most directly comparable with this analysis.
Emissivity measurement Measures the surface emissivity for the radiation at boundaries.

19.3 How the simulator calculates it

It casts a great many rays through the structure.

  1. Rays start from random points in the pores in random directions.
  2. The distance each travels before hitting the solid is recorded.
  3. Tens of thousands of rays give the distribution of distances.
  4. Its mean is the mean free path; its inverse is the extinction coefficient β.
  5. The formula above gives the radiative equivalent conductivity at each temperature.

21. Percolation and passing particle size

Open-source basis: scikit-image distance transform and shortest paths (MCP_Geometric) — own implementation

Answers "how large a particle can pass through this structure?" Used for the largest particle passing a filter, the cut-off of a membrane and the flowability of powders. The result is not only a number: the path the particle actually takes is shown in 3D.

21.1 How it is measured — bubble point

There is a clear counterpart in the laboratory: the bubble point test (ASTM F316).

  1. The pores of the specimen are fully wetted with a liquid (a special liquid of low surface tension).
  2. Gas pressure is slowly raised on one side.
  3. At some pressure the first bubble appears on the other side; the opening it came through is the largest through-pore.
d = 4 γ cos θ / Δp

Raising the pressure further and recording the flow gives the size distribution of the through-pores (capillary flow porometry). This analysis computes exactly that value.

Easily confused with mercury intrusion (chapter 22). Mercury intrusion measures the size distribution of pores that can be entered; the bubble point measures the narrowest throat of a path that goes all the way through. The former includes inner pores, the latter only through-paths, so the bubble-point value always comes out smaller.

21.2 How the simulator calculates it

It asks whether "a ball of radius r can roll through the pores to the other side".

  1. The distance transform is computed: at each point of the pores, the distance to the nearest solid wall. Where it is ≥ r, the centre of a ball of radius r can sit.
  2. Only the region with distance ≥ r is kept.
  3. Whether that region connects one face to the opposite face is checked.
  4. r is reduced from large values to find the largest r that passes.
  5. For each passing size, the shortest path is found and drawn in 3D.

21.5 A judgement by example

In example B (45 % open pores) the median pore diameter of mercury intrusion was 2.46 µm, yet the largest passing sphere comes out smaller. No contradiction —

  • Mercury intrusion counts sizes including wide chambers.
  • The passing size is caught at the narrowest throat of the path.

In short, a wide room with a narrow door cannot be passed. That is why filtration performance must be judged by this value, not by the pore-size distribution.

22. Pore-size distribution (porosimetry)

Open-source code: PoreSpy morphological drainage

How large the pores are, and how many of each size. It imitates in the computer the standard experiment, mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP), so the result comes out in the same form as a measurement.

22.1 How it is measured — mercury intrusion

Mercury wets almost no material, so it does not enter unless pushed. The method uses this.

  1. The specimen is immersed in mercury. At low pressure the mercury does not enter.
  2. Raising the pressure, mercury enters the largest pores first.
  3. Higher pressure pushes it into smaller pores.
  4. The volume of mercury intruded is recorded at each pressure.
d = −4 γ cos θ / p　(Washburn equation)

At pressure p the pores of diameter d are entered, so the pressure–volume curve is the pore-size distribution. γ is the surface tension of mercury, θ the contact angle (usually 130–140°).

Mercury intrusion has a well-known limitation — the ink-bottle effect. When a wide chamber is reached only through a narrow throat, mercury fills the chamber at the pressure that opens the throat, and the chamber's volume is recorded at the throat's size. Small pores seem more numerous than they are, and this calculation has the same property — it imitates the same physics.

22.2 How the simulator calculates it

It does not compute pressures but does the geometrically equivalent thing.

  1. Take a ball of radius r.
  2. Find every place in the pores where it fits (distance transform ≥ r).
  3. Fill only those reachable from outside through connected space — the part that reproduces the ink-bottle effect.
  4. Reducing r step by step accumulates the volume filled at each size.

Because it asks "can it get in from outside", it gives the same result as the intrusion process of the experiment.

22.3 Reading the numbers

Read it as one reads the measured curve.

ItemMeaning and use

Mean pore diameter Volume-weighted mean diameter.
Critical / threshold diameter The diameter at which mercury begins to cross the specimen — the value best correlated with the permeability, close in meaning to the bubble point (chapter 21).
Distribution width Width of the distribution, e.g. d90/d10. Narrow means uniform pores; wide means very mixed sizes.
  • A steep step means many pores of that size.
  • Two steps mean two families of pores of different size (a bimodal pore structure).
  • A curve that does not reach 1 means trapped pores unreachable from outside.

Permeability (chapter 15) Roughly proportional to the square of the critical diameter. A mismatch between the two is a reason to suspect one of them.

Ink-bottle effect kept Not a limitation but a choice to match the experiment. For true chamber sizes, see the pore network.
The structure does not change Real mercury intrusion reaches high pressures (up to 400 MPa) and crushes weak specimens. The calculation has no such effect and may even be more accurate for soft materials.
Voxel resolution Pores smaller than two or three voxels are not caught. Always suspect the small end of the distribution.
No contact angle or surface tension A pure geometric calculation; no mercury properties enter. When comparing with measurements, remember that their diameters depend on the contact angle assumed.

Compare it with the local thickness (chapter 20). Even a wide chamber behind a narrow throat fills only at the throat's diameter, so the values of this map are smaller than or equal to the local thickness. Chambers where the two maps differ greatly are ink-bottle pores — the part read as smaller pore sizes than real in a measured intrusion curve.

23. Pore network

Open-source code: PoreSpy SNOW2 algorithm — divides the pore space into pores and throats

Simplifies a complex pore space into a network of "pores" (chambers) and the "throats" connecting them. Its purpose is to show the connectivity that the pore-size distribution of chapter 22 cannot.

This analysis does not need OpenPNM. The network is extracted by PoreSpy's SNOW2 alone, so the feature works even when OpenPNM is missing from the offline package.

23.1 What the analysis does

A pore space is usually wide chambers linked by narrow throats. As a graph it tells:

  • how many chambers there are and with how many neighbours each connects — connectivity;
  • how much narrower the throats are than the chambers — the degree of bottlenecking;
  • how many dead ends and isolated chambers there are — useless pores.

The extracted network is the input of pore-network modelling (PNM). Phenomena too heavy for voxel solves — two-phase flow (water and air together), drying, capillary invasion — can be solved far faster on the network.

23.2 Relation to experiments

No experiment measures the pore network itself. It is an interpretation, not an observation. Each element has a corresponding measurement, though.

ElementCorresponding measurement
Throat sizeWhat mercury intrusion (chapter 22) and the bubble point (chapter 21) measure is exactly the throats.
Pore sizeEstimated by gas adsorption or X-ray CT image analysis.
Coordination numberHard to measure directly; usually obtained from CT images by the same method as this analysis.

23.3 How the simulator calculates it

  1. The distance transform of the pore space (distance to the wall) is computed.
  2. Local maxima of the distance are taken as pore centres.
  3. A watershed segmentation divides the pore space into pores.
  4. The narrowest cross-section between two touching pores is defined as a throat.
  5. A network is built with pores as nodes and throats as links.

23.4 Reading the numbers

Regions where many large balls connect are the main channels of flow and diffusion; a ball with a single stick is a dead-end pore. The lower the coordination number (sticks per pore), the more easily the flow is cut when a pore clogs. The network can be exported as input to larger-scale pore-network models (flow and adsorption simulations).

25.3 Three things common to every property analysis

(1) Tensor table

A property can differ with direction, so it is given as a 3×3 tensor; the screen shows the diagonal components xx, yy, zz.

  • Three similar values → isotropic: the particles lie at random.
  • One direction larger → anisotropic: particle orientation or connection paths.
  • Isotropic mean — the mean of the three, to compare with measurements that do not distinguish direction.
  • With repeated realisations a 95 % CI appears too. A wide interval signals a small RVE.

(2) Derived numbers

Below the tensor come practical values derived from it: thermal resistance and diffusivity for the thermal conductivity, resistivity and sheet resistance for the electrical conductivity, and so on. Values that need a layer thickness use the thickness entered on screen.

(3) Field statistics per region

The mean and maximum of the computed quantity in each phase (matrix, filler) are tabulated. A maximum far above the mean means local concentration — a clue to where breakdown or cracks start.

29.5 Stage 2 — several objectives at once (NSGA-II)

In real design one usually wants more than one thing, and the wishes conflict. More filler raises the thermal conductivity but adds weight. 29.6 Recommended order

  1. Sweep widely with a DOE (chapter 28).
  2. In stage 1 see which factors matter; if R² is low, try another model.
  3. If the optimum sits at a corner of the range, widen the range and rerun.
  4. Add a few points with Queue the suggested run and retrain.
  5. Once R² is high enough, go to stage 2 and read the optimum or the trade-off front.
  6. Run one more real analysis at the chosen condition to confirm the prediction.

30. Calibration — finding unknown properties from measurements

Own implementation: acoustic and diffuse mismatch theory (AMM/DMM) · effective-medium pre-check (Every / Hasselman–Johnson) · RVE surrogate (Gaussian process) · Bayesian posterior · identifiability analysis (Brun et al. 2001) · next-measurement suggestion (D-optimal)

A specimen of epoxy with 50 vol% alumina measures 1.4 W/m·K. What is the interface thermal resistance between filler and resin? This feature works back from measured effective properties to unknown material values automatically. What theory can settle is left to theory, only the rest is fitted to the measurements — and it tells whether the answer is unique.

30.1 Why it is hard — the answer may not be unique

Finding one interface resistance Rint from one conductivity measurement is not hard. The trouble starts with two or more unknowns. The conductivity kp of a powdered alumina, for example, may be well below the single-crystal value (about 30 W/m·K) and is then unknown too. In the dilute limit, however, the conductivity of the compound does not see these two values separately but only in the combination (Hasselman–Johnson 1987)

kp* = kp / (1 + 2 Rint kp / d)

A large kp with a large Rint and a small pair of both can give the same kp*. With one measurement and two unknowns, the answer is not a point but a curve (a valley). Adding measurements at other contents only measures the same kp* again and does not narrow it. A measurement at another particle size d, by contrast, changes only the influence of Rint and separates the two (the method of Every et al. 1992). This feature judges such situations automatically.

The most important principle is therefore to reduce the unknowns. The resin conductivity is measured on the resin alone; values settled by data sheets, literature, theory or MD/DFT calculations are fixed in the Case. Leave only the one or two values truly unknown, and the measurements can determine them.

30.2 What theory can settle first

Interface thermal resistance has theoretical reference values: the acoustic mismatch model (AMM) and the diffuse mismatch model (DMM), which give the fraction of phonons crossing a perfect interface from the sound velocities (from E, ν, ρ) and heat capacities of the two materials (Swartz, Pohl 1989). The Calibration tab computes them automatically from the materials of the Case.

Example: epoxy / Al₂O₃ValueMeaning
DMM9.6×10⁻⁸ m²K/WResistance when phonons scatter randomly at the interface
AMM2.5×10⁻⁷ m²K/WResistance when the interface is smooth and reflects and transmits like a mirror
Kapitza radius0.019 µmRint × kresin. When this length approaches the particle radius, the interface dominates conduction.
Critical d0.038 µmParticles smaller than this conduct heat worse than the resin itself (Hasselman–Johnson).

Real interfaces, with poor adhesion, silane layers and micro-voids, have resistances larger than or equal to these. They therefore serve as a lower limit or the centre of a prior (use as prior: centred on the DMM value, one decade wide). An interface resistance computed by MD or taken from the literature goes in as a centre value the same way. This program does not run MD/DFT itself — it provides the place to accept their results as priors.

30.3 How to use it

A sample deviating by more than 2σ means no value within the ranges explains that measurement. Widen the ranges, or suspect causes missing from the model — filler agglomeration, voids, interphases, a size distribution different from the real one. A value forced to fit in such a case must not be used. The "Effective medium" column puts the same values into the effective-medium model; its difference from the RVE (4–8 % here) is the effect of the particle arrangement.

  1. Build the Case as the reference. Set the matrix, filler, structure and RVE size as usual; this is the basis of every measured sample.
  2. Enter the measurements (Add measurement): the measured property (thermal or electrical conductivity, permittivity, modulus, CTE), the direction (mean or x/y/z), the value and its uncertainty (±%), and, with Condition, whatever differs from the Case (content, particle size, RVE size …). When the particle size differs a lot, change the RVE and voxel size too, to keep the voxels per particle similar.
  3. Choose the unknowns (Add unknown, at most 3): range and scale (log/linear), prior (uniform in the range, or centre ± width).
  4. Check identifiability: the effective-medium model tells within a second whether "these measurements can determine these unknowns". Check the problem set-up before solving any RVE.
  5. Calibrate on the RVE: solves the real RVE repeatedly to find the answer. Progress appears in the console and the Solver convergence tab.
  6. Read the result (30.5) and put only the determined values into the Case with Apply to the case. Values with only an upper or lower bound, or not determined, stay out.

30.4 How the simulator calculates it

  1. Each sample structure is generated once. The unknowns change only material values, so the structure stays. Structures identical to a previous run in the same work folder are reused.
  2. Design runs: every sample is solved on the RVE at 13 combinations within the ranges of the unknowns (for two unknowns). They are independent and run on several cores at once.
  3. Surrogate: for each sample, a Gaussian process fits the log of the result as a function of the unknowns (log scale) (Kennedy, O'Hagan 2001).
  4. Posterior: prior × likelihood on a fine grid (161×161 for two unknowns). The likelihood includes the measurement uncertainty, the error of the RVE itself (2 % by default) and the surrogate error.
  5. Refinement: more RVE solves where the posterior concentrates and the surrogate is uncertain, until the surrogate error at the estimate is below 0.2 times the measurement uncertainty.
  6. Verification solves: every sample is solved again directly on the RVE with the values found — the "RVE at the estimate" of the result table.
  7. Identifiability and next measurement: the sensitivity matrix of the measurements to the unknowns at the estimate shows which combinations are determined; among candidate measurements with other sizes and contents, the one adding the most information is suggested.

30.7 The same material measured at several contents

The most common experimental data are several specimens of the same filler and resin at different contents (e.g. 20, 35, 50 vol%). Enter them like this:

  1. Build the Case for one of them (e.g. 35 vol%).
  2. Add each measurement as a row with Add measurement; for the other contents change only …: content under Condition. Use the scatter of repeated measurements as the uncertainty.

The calculation then proceeds as follows:

  • An RVE structure is generated for each content (once each, then reused). A different content changes the distances and contacts between particles, so the same structure cannot serve.
  • At each combination of unknowns all specimens are solved together, and a surrogate is built per specimen.
  • The likelihood is the product over all measurements: the answer sought explains every content with one set of values. Specimens with smaller measurement uncertainty weigh more.
  • Each specimen is solved again with the values found and its deviation shown. If one content alone deviates strongly, a phenomenon acting only there — particle contact at high content (Rc), agglomeration, voids, percolation — is missing from the model, and the result warns about it.

A complete example finding Rint and kp from three specimens (20, 35, 50 vol%) and one of another particle size — specimen structures, what becomes determined as measurements are added, the result screens and what the interface resistance does to the heat flow in the structure — is in section 32.2.

In summary:

  • With one unknown, measuring several contents is the best approach. The interval narrows, and whether one value fits every content is itself a check of the model.
  • With two unknowns (Rint and kp), contents alone are not enough: in the dilute limit the conductivity sees only the combination kp/(1 + 2Rintkp/d) (30.1). Particle interactions at high content give slightly different information, so kp is partly determined, but Rint only gets an upper bound.
  • One more specimen of another particle size separates them. What to measure next in the result puts the same conclusion (a smaller size, 0.15 µm) at the top.
  • When high-content specimens with touching particles are included, the contact resistance Rc acts too. Then make Rint and Rc both unknowns, and let low-content specimens with hardly any contact determine Rint and high-content ones Rc.

30.8 Points of caution

The values found reproduce the measurements "within this RVE model". If the real specimen has agglomeration, voids, a different size distribution or a filler surface layer that the model lacks, the difference is absorbed into the unknowns. For a specimen whose conductivity is high because of agglomeration, for example, the interface resistance found comes out smaller than the real one. Make the structure (size distribution, content, porosity) as close to the measured one as possible.
  • Enter honest uncertainties. ±0.1 % for a value with ±2 % repeatability makes the intervals unrealistically narrow and ignores the model error. Adjust the RVE model uncertainty (2 % by default) to the RVE size and the number of realisations (chapters 6 and 31).
  • Results stuck at a range limit must be checked again with a wider range. There is no need to widen into physically meaningless ranges (negative resistance, k above the single crystal).
  • At most 3 unknowns. Determining more from a few effective-property measurements is nearly impossible; design more independent measurements (other sizes, other properties) first.
  • In highly filled specimens with touching particles, Rint and the contact resistance Rc act together. To find both, measure low-filled samples with hardly any contact (Rint only) together with highly filled ones.

A real example: example A of this guide (epoxy + AlN 40 vol%, diameter 3 µm, voxel 0.4 µm) ended with 28 passed, 2 warnings, 0 failed. The two warnings report the same fact twice.

① "Voxel size that keeps every particle apart 0.4 µm (limit 0.217 µm)"

A planning check issued before the analysis. Keeping a 2-voxel gap between particles needs voxels of 0.217 µm or less (14 voxels per diameter); with 0.4 µm, some particles will touch on the grid (section 5.5).

② "Touching particles 819 (limit 0)"

In the structure actually generated, 819 pairs of voxels of different particles were neighbours, none of them across a face. All only graze at edges or corners, and the default calculation (FV) passes no heat there. Because particles touched on the grid, the resolution check ran automatically: on a 1.5× finer grid the thermal conductivity changed by only −0.27 % and the permittivity by +0.34 %. Despite the two warnings, the results are stable with respect to resolution.

What to do — for values to report, reduce the voxel size with Standard quality or above. For comparisons and trends, they can be used as they are as long as the resolution check changes by only a few per cent.

31.4 A real example with failures — example C2

Warnings alone do not convey the feel, so here is an example that actually failed. Example C2 of this guide (epoxy + carbonyl iron 35 vol%, overlapping particles allowed) ended with 31 passed, 4 warnings, 2 failed.

Failed itemValue / limit
Thermal conductivity — Directional scatter of an isotropic structure0.254 / 0.05
Electrical conductivity — Directional scatter of an isotropic structure0.464 / 0.05

A structure that should be isotropic, yet its x, y, z values scatter by 25 % and 46 %. That does not mean the calculation is wrong.

C2 sits near the percolation threshold, where the filler is barely connected and conduction has just begun. In such a structure the conduction paths consist of a few chains that differ per direction, so a thin path in x and thick paths in y and z really occur (thermal conductivity x 3.01, y 3.91, z 3.92 W/m·K). The scatter itself is the physics of that structure.

So what should be done — do not read the values as three, "x is this, y is that". Read them as one value with a large uncertainty. For a proper number, enlarge the RVE or raise the Realisations to obtain the mean and confidence interval over several seeds. Near percolation this is the only correct way.

The four warnings of the same example

Two of the warnings are resolution checks. With 66,340 touching voxel pairs on the grid (11,103 across faces) the check ran automatically; on a 1.5× finer grid the thermal conductivity changed by −3.3 % and the electrical conductivity by −16 %. The area represented by one voxel face at a contact shrinks as the grid gets finer, so conduction through contacts is sensitive to resolution. The other two are the number of particle contacts and the directional deviation of the permeability (9 %).

The electrical conductivity of example C2, 3.2×10⁵ S/m, must therefore be read to an order of magnitude only (the extrapolated value of the resolution check is 1.2×10⁵ S/m). The real information it carries is not the number but the fact that "it became a conductor, not an insulator", which gains meaning only beside the 2.4×10⁻¹³ S/m of example C, whose filler is disconnected. That is why this guide uses C2 — to show that what decides whether shielding works is connectivity, not content.

31.5 Bounds — is the value physically possible?

Conduction and thermal-expansion results come with a Bounds and mean-field models plot. The Wiener and Hashin–Shtrikman bounds are limits that cannot be crossed, set by the volume fractions alone. A value outside the Wiener bounds is recorded as failed (an error); one more than 1 % outside the Hashin–Shtrikman bounds as a warning. The HS bounds hold for statistically isotropic infinite composites, so a finite voxel RVE may stray slightly (the example in section 9.5).

Differences from approximate models such as Maxwell-Garnett or Bruggeman, on the other hand, are normal: the models know nothing of particle orientation, contact or agglomeration — and that difference is the reason to use this program.

31.6 When the result differs from the measurement

The computed value usually comes out better than the measured one: the calculation lacks the imperfections of a real part.

Present in reality, absent in the calculationEffect
Interface thermal resistance / debondingLowers the thermal conductivity. Can be entered as Rint (filler–matrix) and Rc (filler–filler) (sections 5.7, 7.6).
Bubbles and voidsLower every property. Can be included as a pore phase.
Filler agglomerationPoor dispersion lowers performance. Can be approximated by allowing overlap.

Recommended use — rather than trying to match absolute values, take one experimentally verified condition as a reference and compare relative changes from it. That comparison is where the calculation is most accurate. Knowing the ratio between reference and calculation, the calculated values of other conditions can be corrected by the same ratio to predict measurements.

31.7 Summary — the order on receiving a result

  1. Look at the Verification tab first. Values with failures are not used.
  2. Read the warnings. Resolution-check changes (and, with the comparison on, FE/FV differences) and directional scatter point to resolution or RVE problems.
  3. Check the position in the bounds plot — inside the rigorous bounds?
  4. Only then read the numbers.
  5. For values to report, run once more at a higher accuracy level to confirm the value is stable.

32.1 Example A — flowability of a MUF (molded underfill)

The problem

A MUF does the moulding over the chip and the underfill between the bumps in one step. Lowering the thermal expansion needs a lot of silica filler; filling between the bumps without voids needs good flow. The goal is to find where these two demands collide.

InputValue
ResinEpoxy, Carreau model at the moulding temperature (μ₀ 2 Pa·s, μ∞ 0.2 Pa·s, λ 0.05 s, n 0.6) — 1.92 Pa·s at 10 1/s
FillerSpherical fused silica: monomodal 5 µm (30 and 55 vol%) / bimodal 15 + 3 µm (70/30, 55 vol%)
AnalysisSimulation → Viscosity and flowability, reference shear rate 10 1/s, bump gap 50 µm, flow length 10 mm
RVEMonomodal 5 µm: 30 µm, voxel 0.375 µm (80³) · bimodal: 60 µm, voxel 0.5 µm (120³)

Result 1 — where and how the resin flows

The viscosity is decided in the end by how hard the resin between the fillers is sheared. The screen shows this in three ways.

In simple shear the upper layers slide towards +x and the lower ones towards −x. The fillers, as rigid bodies, rotate as a whole (circular streamlines inside the fillers), and the resin goes around them and escapes through the gaps. Near the mid-height (the plane of zero velocity) the resin wraps around the fillers in vortices. Where the colour is red — gaps where two fillers face each other closely — the resin is sheared several times harder than average, spends that much more energy, and the compound becomes more viscous.

The dye lines tilt with the shear and bend like steps where they pass a filler. The filler is rigid and does not deform, so to move the same distance the resin between fillers must deform for it too. This shows by eye why fillers raise the viscosity, from Einstein's 2.5φ to the divergence of Krieger–Dougherty.

Animated resin flow in the Result Viewer moves tracers along the streamlines inside the translucent structure in the 3D view. Upper and lower layers slide in opposite directions, tracers at mid-height almost stand still, and tracers passing between fillers speed up and slow down. The lines themselves are coloured by velocity with the colour bar at the top right, so fast and slow layers are distinguishable even in a paused view or a report figure. The "flow" that still pictures convey poorly can be shown in a meeting as it is.

At 55 vol% the picture changes. The resin remains only as a network of thin films between fillers, each strongly sheared (mean amplification 7.0, against 2.0 at 30 vol%). In the same section the dye lines bend almost everywhere. A little more content and the films break, the particles interlock and nothing flows — the maximum packing fraction φm is close. The RVE voxels can no longer resolve the thin films (24,220 particle contacts), so the program uses Krieger–Dougherty as the best value instead of the RVE value (164).

32.2 Example B — interface thermal resistance from measurements at several contents

The problem

The thermal conductivity was measured on three specimens of epoxy with 0.5 µm fused silica (20, 35, 50 vol%) and one with 2 µm silica (35 vol%). Unknown are the interface thermal resistance Rint between filler and resin and the conductivity kp of this silica grade (whether the data-sheet 1.38 W/m·K holds for this powder is uncertain). Here the "measurements" were made by solving the RVE with true values Rint = 1.0×10⁻⁷ m²K/W and kp = 1.2 W/m·K plus a scatter within ±1.5 %, so the method can be checked against a known answer. The fifth specimen is the one the program suggested after seeing the first four (step 3).

Step 1 — theory first

The Calibration tab shows the interface resistance of a perfect interface from the sound velocities and heat capacities of the two materials: for epoxy/silica, DMM 3.5×10⁻⁸ and AMM 3.9×10⁻⁸ m²K/W. Real interfaces have larger or equal values, so the range of the unknown is set wide, 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁵, with this value as the reference for the lower limit. The value finally found (about 1×10⁻⁷) is about 3 times the theoretical lower limit — a common size for real interfaces with imperfect adhesion.

Measurements varying only the content cannot fully separate kp and Rint: in the dilute limit the conductivity sees only the combination kp/(1 + 2Rintkp/d). A specimen with larger particles (2 µm), where the interface weighs less, pins kp, but the lower side of Rint stays open: with 0.5 µm particles, Rint = 2×10⁻⁸ gives 2Rintkp/d of only 0.1, indistinguishable from "no interface resistance" within the measurement scatter. One more specimen with small particles (0.15 µm, where the same Rint gives 2Rintkp/d = 1.6), in which the interface weighs heavily, closes the lower side too, and both values are determined in narrow intervals containing the true values.

Step 3 — reading the result screen, and the next measurement

A valley means "these measurements cannot separate this direction". The collinearity index γ (4.0) of the Identifiability box is computed from the slopes near the estimate and does not see a plateau running to the end of the range. The program therefore labels a posterior that does not fall to 0 at an end of the range as Upper bound/Lower bound, stating which end and the most probable value.

Step 4 — one more specimen, as suggested

Entering the measurement of the suggested specimen (50 vol%, d 0.15 µm), 0.3188 W/m·K, as the fifth and running again (about 1 minute) gives the last row of the step-2 table. The Rint interval closes to 5.4×10⁻⁸ – 1.4×10⁻⁷ (×9.1 against the prior) and kp narrows to 0.93 – 1.55 (×8.7), both containing the true values (1.0×10⁻⁷, 1.2).

Step 5 — what the interface resistance does inside the structure

Without interface resistance (left), heat passes through the more conductive silica like stepping stones, and the flux inside the particles is large. With Rint = 1×10⁻⁷ (middle) heat is reluctant to enter the particles: the flux inside them drops by 16 % and the total flux (the conductivity) falls by 12 %, from 0.366 to 0.321 W/m·K. The graph on the right shows why. The temperature step across one voxel at a particle boundary grows from 17.5 to 23.9 mK — the added 6.4 mK is the temperature that jumps like a step at the boundary, ΔT = Rint·q (q ≈ 6×10⁴ W/m²). Less temperature difference remains across the particle (8.0 → 6.8 mK), and the particle carries less heat. Smaller particles have more interface per volume and a larger effect — the figure shows why specimens of other sizes, especially small ones, separate Rint.

Summary

  • To find the interface resistance alone, measurements of the same material at several contents suffice; more contents narrow the interval and check the model.
  • If the conductivity of the filler grade is unknown too, specimens of other particle sizes are needed: large sizes pin kp, small sizes Rint (especially its lower side). Calibration tells this before measuring with Check identifiability, and after measuring suggests which specimen to make next.
  • Read the badges as they are: Determined is a value, Upper bound/Lower bound only one limit, Not determined means the measurements do not respond to that unknown.
  • With the values put into the Case by Apply to the case, that interface resistance predicts the conductivity at other contents and particle sizes.

C.3 Taking the structure to another solver — comparison calculations

Structure TIFF set in the Results tab downloads structure.tif, structure_labels.tif and structure_legend.csv in one ZIP. To solve the same RVE in another voxel-based program and compare results:

  1. Import the structure — read structure.tif (grey levels) as an image stack and assign a material ID to each grey value of the legend. An import that takes values directly as material numbers reads structure_labels.tif.
  2. Voxel size — enter voxel_size_um from the legend (it is also in the ImageJ resolution of the TIFF).
  3. Material properties — assign k, εr, E, ν, α and so on from the legend as they are. Regions with void = 1 are pores (gas).
  4. Boundary conditions — this program solves with periodic conditions in every direction; use periodic conditions in the comparison too. If interface resistances (Rint, Rc) were used, give the other program the same interface conditions.
Following the ImageJ convention, the axis order inside the file is (z, y, x) — the first image is the z = 0 section. With the same grid and voxel size, the structures in both programs match voxel for voxel. In v3, structure.tif held only region numbers (0, 1, 2 …) and looked black in viewers; since v4 grey levels and the label map are written separately.

C.4 Output of a DOE study

A DOE creates one such folder per case, named with the factor values that case changed — e.g. doe-HGM-t-0.526817-vf-29.1782 (thickness 0.53 µm, content 29.2 vol%). The DOE cases gather under the case folder that was open when the study was created. For a summary of the whole study, the CSV download of the DOE tab is most convenient — a table with factors and responses on one row.

Appendix D. Troubleshooting

Situations met often in practice, as symptom → cause → remedy. When a problem occurs, open the run.log of the run folder first.

D.1 The program does not start

SymptomCause and remedy
A window opens but stays blank The browser cannot reach the server. Another program may be using the same port. Check with 4_NETWORK_CHECK.bat.
"ImportError: vtkCapsuleSource" A VTK version conflict. PuMA must be used with vtk 9.5.2; a newer vtk (9.7) breaks both PyVista and PuMA. Always pin vtk=9.5.2 and pyvista=0.47.1 when creating the environment.
Silent exit on import porespy
(no error message)		 A DLL was not found. The Library\bin of the PuMA environment must be on the PATH; in the distribution _common.bat restores it automatically.
The PuMA environment is not found 0_CHECK_ENVIRONMENT.bat reports which environment was found.
The installation tries to download packages The offline installation must run with --no-index. If it reaches for the internet, that package is missing from the wheels\ folder.
numpy errors after installation numpy 2.x breaks PuMA. Check that constraints-offline.txt pins numpy<2 and scipy 1.11.
A run is missing from the Runs list Each browser keeps its own mark in a cookie and sees only its own runs. Runs started with 6_RUN_COMMANDLINE.bat or scripts, on the other hand, have no owner and are visible to every user. If a run is still missing, it was started in another browser (or a private window); check there, or open the work folder directly (Appendix C).
Right after installation, run 5_SELF_TEST.bat once. It solves 54 problems with known answers (38 in the quick check) to confirm the engine works (chapter 31).

D.2 Generating structures

SymptomCause and remedy
Structure generation fails The requested content cannot be packed geometrically — frequent with small particles at high contents. Choose one of ① allow overlap, ② reduce the minimum gap, ③ lower the content.
The actual content is below the target Not all particles fitted. Check the actual volume fraction in the structure analysis (chapter 20) and interpret the properties on that actual value.
Volume-fraction error warning Comes from approximating shapes by voxels and shrinks with smaller voxels. Around 3 % it has practically no effect.
Results change with the seed Too few particles for stable statistics. Enlarge the RVE, or run the same condition with several seeds and look at the mean and spread.

D.3 During the analysis

SymptomCause and remedy
"Solver did not converge" The iterative solution did not reach the target residual. Do not use the value. It happens with extreme property contrasts (10¹⁰ or more) or coarse grids. Raise the resolution or adjust the tolerance.
The elastic analysis takes too long It solves seven loads and is the heaviest (chapter 12). Run it alone at a low resolution first to see the trend.
Out of memory Reduce the grid. Memory scales with the number of voxels: doubling the grid edge needs 8 times as much.
Only some DOE cases fail Usually structure generation failures; see section 28.4. Failed cases are excluded from the optimisation training.

D.4 Strange results

SymptomCause and remedy
The permeability is 0 The pores are not connected in that direction. Look at the open porosity of the structure analysis and the Connected flag of the percolation (chapters 20, 21). Even a high porosity passes no fluid if the pores are trapped.
Electrical conductivity at insulator level The filler is not connected. Look at Clusters and Percolating of the grain analysis (chapter 24). Allowing overlap or high-aspect-ratio fillers work better than more content (chapter 8).
EMI shielding is 0 dB Because the electrical conductivity is 0. Solve the item above first (chapter 11).
Shielding above 1000 dB A value the formula produces. Real measurements are limited to about 100 dB, and in real parts leakage at seams dominates. Read 80 dB or more as "sufficient" (chapter 11).
FE and FV values differ greatly Happens with large property contrasts and coarse grids. Rerun at a higher resolution. If used as they are, allow for an uncertainty of that size (chapter 31).
An isotropic structure scatters between directions A small RVE or few particles. Near the percolation threshold it may be normal physics (section 31.4).
A value lies outside the rigorous bounds Not a warning but an error. Do not use the value; check the property input and the resolution.
The computed value is better than the measured one Normal: the calculation lacks interface resistance, bubbles and agglomeration. See the table in section 31.6.

D.5 On screen

SymptomCause and remedy
Arrows or streamlines are invisible Field on the RVE faces hides them. Switch it off or lower the opacity (chapter 26).
Only a few streamlines are drawn The voxels are coarse for the pore width, so the velocity near the walls is almost 0; a higher resolution gives more. With too few crossing lines, none are drawn, to avoid misleading.
Colours crowd to one end and hide the structure A wide value range (current density and the like). Switch on Log.
Several conditions are hard to compare Fix the colour range to the same values with Manual.
The particle on a path looks larger than real It is drawn enlarged for visibility; a note at the bottom of the view says so (chapter 21).

  2. Check the results of 0_CHECK_ENVIRONMENT.bat and 5_SELF_TEST.bat together.
  3. Whether the same problem occurs on a smaller grid too narrows down the cause.
  4. When asking for help, send spec.normalized.json and run.log together; with these two the conditions can be reproduced exactly.
